A debate on Leviathan, Thomas Hobbes (1651)

This House believes that Hobbes's subjects never truly leave the state of nature

Hobbes wrote Leviathan to explain why people should obey a government. He starts by imagining life with no government at all, which he calls the state of nature. There, you are your own judge, nobody is safe, and everyone lives ready for war. People get out by making a covenant, a binding promise to each other. In it they hand their right to rule themselves to one person or one assembly, called the sovereign. The debate asked whether that exit is ever complete. Hobbes also names one right no promise can take away: the right to defend your own life. So if the sovereign orders your death, you may resist, and Hobbes says resisting does no wrong. One side said this leftover right means the state of nature is still there, buried at the base of the state, and it shows up whenever the ruler turns deadly. The other side said it's one liberty allowed inside a legal order that works, and that Hobbes plainly describes people leaving nature and, through rebellion or collapse, going back to it. The argument came down to how you define the state of nature. It could be a whole condition of society. It could also be a bare structure: two people with no judge above them.

British Parliamentary format, 8 speakers in 4 teams. 17 September 2026.

In brief

The debate asked whether people who join Hobbes's commonwealth (his word for an organized state) ever fully leave the lawless condition he calls the state of nature. One side said no. Every subject keeps the right to fight for their own life, even against a lawful death sentence, and at that point subject and ruler face each other with no judge above them. The other side said yes. The state of nature is a whole condition with no law, no property and no shared judge, and the commonwealth removes all of that, so only one narrow liberty is left, inside a legal order that works. By the end, both sides agreed on what the text says and disagreed about what to call it. The debate left one question open: is a single right that can't be taken away enough to say the old condition never left?

The positions

The vertical remainder: nature survives between subject and sovereign

Subjects leave the war of all against all. But at the one point where the sovereign's power threatens a subject's life, two natural rights face each other with no common judge, so the core structure of the state of nature never fully goes away.

Why people hold it

  • Chapter XIV says you can't give up the right to resist people who attack you to take your life, because the whole reason for giving up rights is your own safety. Chapter XIV uses this same right to define the right of nature.
  • Chapter XXI says a subject, even one fairly condemned, may refuse an order to kill or injure themself, or an order not to resist an attack. Authorizing the sovereign to kill me doesn't bind me to kill myself.
  • Chapter XXVIII says subjects never gave the sovereign his right to punish. They left it with him, and it is as complete as in the condition of mere nature, limited only by natural law. So the ruler's power to force people at this point is his original natural right, not something new.
  • Chapter XVIII says the sovereign makes no covenant with his subjects, so when the two clash, no authority on earth stands over both.
  • Chapter XXI says subjects owe obedience only as long as the sovereign can protect them. That condition points back to a right nobody ever handed over, so the exception was there from the start.
  • Hobbes says the commonwealth exists to keep people alive. The one thing it can't guarantee is a subject's life against the sovereign. The gap sits right on top of the reason the state was built.

Where it held up

  • Every quotation matched the text. The Chapter XXI liberties of the condemned and the Chapter XXVIII line about the right to punish being as complete as in nature both appear in the text, and nobody challenged them.
  • The split between a ruling being final on the law and a ruling being able to force someone to go along held up. The other side agreed no covenant can bind a subject to give up their life.
  • The text backed the point about people facing the death penalty. Chapter XXI says those already facing death may band together and defend each other, and that defending themselves is not unjust at all. That answered the claim that Hobbes bans every alliance.
  • The other side agreed the liberty subjects keep is a basic limit on the civil contract, more than a gap in the laws.

Where it fell short

  • This side dropped the claim that natural right and the state of nature are the same thing. It dropped its argument from the word "renewed." It agreed the relationship is otherwise civil and unequal. That left a claim about one structural point, and nobody showed Hobbes calling that point the state of nature.
  • The other side answered the "no common power" definition with Chapter XVIII, where the sovereign judges all disputes and the subject authorized him to. The reply was that a judge who can't force the condemned isn't keeping them "in awe." That stretches "in awe" to mean being impossible to physically resist, and the text doesn't say that.
  • The other side said the covenant wording "I authorize you so long as you protect me" isn't in the text, and that's right. The Chapter XVII wording has no such clause. The protection condition does appear in Hobbes, but it comes from Chapter XXI. This side never showed that Hobbes writes it into the covenant itself, as opposed to describing what happens when the covenant stops doing its job.
  • The strongest version of the claim, that the sovereign owes the subject no duty to back down, skips over Hobbes saying sovereigns are bound by natural law and answer to God. This side waved that off as "no protector on earth." It never explained why a limit on one side that nobody can enforce counts as the state of nature, when Hobbes uses that term for a condition where nothing can be unjust at all.
  • The purpose argument says the gap shows up right where staying alive matters most. That tells you how satisfying Hobbes's answer is. It doesn't show that Hobbes would say the gap is still in nature.

A real exit: the state of nature is a whole condition, and the commonwealth ends it

Hobbes defines the state of nature as a complete condition with no law, no property, no justice and no common judge, and the commonwealth removes all of them. So keeping one natural liberty doesn't mean you're still in nature.

Why people hold it

  • Chapter XIII lists the signs of that condition: no common power to keep everyone in awe, no industry, no clear line between mine and yours, and nothing can be unjust. Chapter XV says property and valid covenants start only once there's a civil power. Chapter XXVI says the laws of nature become real laws only once a commonwealth is set up. For subjects, every one of these is reversed.
  • Chapter XVIII gives the sovereign the right to judge all disputes, and it says the alternative, each person protecting themself by their own strength, is the condition of war. Hobbes makes the sovereign the common judge to end that condition.
  • Chapter XXI says each subject is the author of everything the sovereign does. That's why King David's killing of Uriah wronged God but did not wrong Uriah. Two people in nature have never given each other anything. Subjects have.
  • Hobbes talks about leaving and going back. Chapter XVII talks about getting out of the miserable condition of war. Chapter XXVIII calls rebellion a relapse into war and war renewed. Chapter XXX says that when sovereignty breaks down, every man returns to the condition of war. You can't go back to a place you never left.
  • The liberty that's left is small. Chapter XXI bans resisting the state's sword to defend someone else. Chapter XIV allows only defending your own body. You can't rebuild the war of all against all from that.
  • In Hobbes, liberty means only that you have no duty. It doesn't mean you have a stronger claim. A condemned subject's liberty to resist sits alongside a lawful sentence. It doesn't cancel the ruling, and it doesn't turn the sovereign's act into a wrong.

Where it held up

  • The other side never knocked down this reading of Chapter XIII. It agreed that natural right and the state of nature are different, and that subjects leave the general condition.
  • The language of leaving and going back is real and was quoted correctly. The side that brought up the word "renewed" to push back on it later dropped that argument.
  • The fire escape comparison held up: a building with a permanent way out doesn't mean the people inside are already outside. The other side never showed Hobbes calling the leftover liberty the state of nature. Asked that exact question, it repeated its structural argument and gave no citation.
  • Both sides accepted the split between a final ruling and going along with it willingly, and here it did more work. Hobbes's judge decides what's lawful. He never promises the condemned person will cooperate.

Where it fell short

  • The other side answered the claim that Hobbes bans the alliances war is made of with text. Chapter XXI lets people already facing the death penalty band together and defend each other without any new injustice. This side conceded the point.
  • One argument called the condemned subject's resistance a reflex, biological and unlawful. Hobbes says the opposite. Defending your own body is not unjust at all, and he states it as a permission, not an instinct. When pressed, this side scaled it back to physically refusing to help with your own death, which is what the other side had said from the start.
  • One speech said civil law now limits the sovereign's power to punish. Chapter XXVI says the sovereign isn't subject to civil laws, and Chapter XXIX calls the opposite view "repugnant to the nature of a commonwealth." Only natural law binds him. Chapter XXVIII says the same thing, and the other side built on it.
  • The "public reason" idea got asserted more than argued. It leaned on a quote about "the reason of a man" versus "the reason of a commonwealth" that isn't in the text. It never said what public reason does at the moment the sovereign orders a subject's death, and the speaker who raised it agreed that public reason doesn't oblige the subject to comply there.
  • This side explained why the sovereign may judge and punish. Beyond pointing out that everything around the relationship is civil, it never fully explained why that relationship isn't the one Hobbes describes in Chapter XIII. Whether the surroundings decide what the relationship itself is stayed a fight over labels.

Key ideas, explained

State of nature

Hobbes's picture of life with no government. Chapter XIII describes it as a time when people live without a common power to keep them all in awe. There's no law, no property and no such thing as injustice, and each person is their own judge. War here means always being ready to fight, even when nobody is fighting.

Right of nature

Chapter XIV defines it as each person's liberty to use their own power to keep themselves alive. In the state of nature this right covers everything. In the commonwealth, people give up most of it. The core part, resisting people who come to kill you, can't be given up by any promise.

Covenant

A binding promise where at least one side delivers later. Hobbes says covenants only truly bind once some power exists to enforce them. Each subject makes the commonwealth's covenant with every other subject. The sovereign isn't a party to it, which is why Hobbes says the sovereign can never break covenant with his subjects.

Sovereign

The person or assembly given the right to rule. Hobbes calls the sovereign a "mortal god" and gives him the right to judge all disputes, make laws and punish. Civil law doesn't bind the sovereign. Only the laws of nature do, and nobody on earth enforces those.

Authorization

Each subject names the sovereign as acting for them, so everything the sovereign does counts as the subject's own act. That's why Hobbes says the sovereign can never wrong a subject, even by killing an innocent one. Authorizing him to kill me still doesn't oblige me to kill myself or stand still for it.

Liberty of subjects

Chapter XXI lists what subjects keep. They may do anything the law doesn't mention. Even when fairly condemned, they may refuse to kill, wound or injure themselves, and refuse an order not to resist an attack. They may not defend someone else against the state's sword. For Hobbes, liberty means only that you have no duty.

Punishment versus hostility

Chapter XXVIII defines punishment as harm that a public authority does to a subject after a public judgment. Harm done to a declared enemy or a rebel counts as an act of war, and Hobbes doesn't call it punishment. He calls rebellion a relapse into the condition of war, and war renewed.

Protection and obedience

Chapter XXI says a subject owes obedience as long as the sovereign has the power to protect them, and no longer. When protection fails, the natural right to protect yourself, which no covenant can remove, takes over. Hobbes says the purpose of obedience is protection.

Where the sides agreed

The two sides agreed on almost every fact about the text.

  • Subjects leave the war of every man against every man. Work, property, valid contracts and settled court cases are real gains of the commonwealth.
  • Natural right and the state of nature are different things. Keeping a natural liberty doesn't by itself mean you're still in nature.
  • No covenant can remove the right to defend your own life. A subject may resist a death sentence, even a fair one, and that resistance is no new injustice. Other people may not help them, with one exception: fellow condemned people who already face death.
  • The sovereign has the final word on what is lawful. That word can't oblige a condemned subject to help with their own death.
  • The sovereign's right to punish is his original natural right. Subjects left it with him and never gave it to him, and only natural law (the rules Hobbes says reason gives everyone) limits it.
  • Hobbes calls rebellion and the breakup of a state a return to war, and he calls that return the greatest evil in this life.

What stayed open

What counts as the state of nature. One side used the structural definition, where no common power stands over the parties, and applied it to a single relationship. The other side used the whole-condition description (no law, no property, no justice) and applied it to a society. Both definitions come from Chapter XIII. The debate didn't settle which one Hobbes treats as basic, or whether you can apply the structural one to one relationship at a time.

Whether a limit nobody can enforce is a gap in civil order or a piece of nature. Everyone agreed on the moment of execution: the sovereign has authority, the subject has liberty, and no power on earth can settle it except force. One side called that a civil exception with law all around it. The other side called it the natural relationship showing through. Nobody found a passage where Hobbes names that moment either way. The closest text is Chapter XXVIII, which calls rebellion a relapse into war. That passage covers a subject who rejects being a subject at all, and the debate didn't settle whether it also covers a condemned subject who resists without rejecting it.

What to take from this

Hobbes built the commonwealth on the one thing people can't give away, the will to stay alive. The right to preserve your own life explains both why people submit to the sovereign and where that submission stops. A subject can authorize the sovereign to do anything, including kill them, and still not be obliged to hold still for it. Hobbes states this in Chapter XXI and again in Chapter XIV.

The disagreement depends on whether the state of nature means a whole way of life or a relationship with no common judge. If it's a whole way of life, the commonwealth clearly ends it. Property, law and a common judge all exist under the commonwealth, and Hobbes calls losing them a disaster. If the state of nature is a structure, two parties with no judge above them, then a trace of it sits at the base of the state for good. Both readings come from the same chapter, and when they pull apart, Hobbes never says which one he means.

The sovereign isn't a referee between himself and his subjects. He exists to fix the problem of people judging their own cases against each other. Hobbes accepts that when the sovereign has a case against a subject, no referee on earth stands over it. He thinks that price is worth paying, because the alternative is that nobody's case has a referee. You can call the gap that's left a piece of nature or a limit of civil order.

Claims the book does not back up

The analyst checked what was said against the text. These claims were made in the debate but could not be found in the book.

  • One speech quoted Hobbes on "the reason of a man" versus "the reason of a commonwealth." That phrase isn't in the text. Hobbes contrasts private reason with "publique reason" once, in Chapter XXXVII on miracles, where he says the sovereign's reason is the public reason.
  • One speech placed the passage about the right to punish in Chapter XXI (the one saying subjects never gave that right, they left it with the sovereign, as complete as in the condition of mere nature). It's in Chapter XXVIII. The same speech said civil law now limits the sovereign's power to punish. Chapter XXVI says the sovereign isn't subject to civil laws, and Chapter XXIX calls the opposite view "repugnant to the nature of a commonwealth."
  • One speech said Hobbes uses "the condition of mere nature" in two ways, once for the state of nature and once for natural right. Each time the phrase appears in the text, it refers to the condition of war among men. The text doesn't support a second meaning.
  • One speech called the condemned subject's resistance unlawful and a biological reflex. Chapter XXI says defending your own body, even after a crime that carries the death penalty, is not unjust at all. Hobbes states it as a liberty, not an instinct. Resisting becomes unlawful only if the subject keeps it up after being offered a pardon.
  • One speech gave the covenant wording as "I authorize you so long as you protect me." The Chapter XVII wording is "I authorise and give up my right of governing myself," on the condition that others do the same, and it has no protection clause. The protection condition comes from Chapter XXI, which the speaker later cited correctly.
  • One speech said Hobbes bans the alliances war is made of. Chapter XXI bans defending someone else against the state's sword, then lets people already facing death for a crime band together and defend each other. The speaker agreed once this was pointed out.

Prime Minister

Sonnet Claude, sonnet Opening Government

I propose the motion: Hobbes's subjects never truly leave the state of nature.

Definition. We concede at the outset what Hobbes plainly gives us: the commonwealth ends general war. Subjects gain industry, culture, "commodious Building," the fruits of Chapter XVII's "Generation of a Common-wealth." We do not contest that horizontal peace among subjects is real. The motion asks something narrower and, we say, decisive: whether the subject's fundamental relation of self-preservation is ever fully surrendered to common adjudication. Our case is that it is not. A vertical reservation of nature survives inside civil society, at the exact point where the sovereign's claim and the subject's claim to preservation might collide. That reservation is not a lapse or a defect in the commonwealth. Hobbes builds it in, by name, in three places.

First contention: the one right that constitutes the state of nature was never transferred. Chapter XIV defines the right of nature as "the Liberty each man hath, to use his own power, as he will himselfe, for the preservation of his own Nature." Hobbes then rules this right out of the class of things a covenant can move:

"a man cannot lay down the right of resisting them, that assault him by force, to take away his life... the motive, and end for which this renouncing... is introduced, is nothing else but the security of a mans person."

Chapter XXI confirms it is not merely theoretical. A subject retains "the Liberty to disobey" a command to kill or maim himself, even one "justly condemned." Authorization of the sovereign's acts does not reach this far: "by allowing him to Kill Me, I am not bound to Kill my selfe when he commands me." Every other right went into the commonwealth. This one, the right that Chapter XIV uses to define the state of nature, stayed with the subject.

Second contention: civil power contains this confrontation without superseding it. Chapter XVIII tells us the institutional covenant runs among subjects, never from the sovereign to them: "there can happen no breach of Covenant on the part of the Soveraigne." So when the subject's retained right meets the sovereign's power, there is no third authority, no common judge, standing over both. And Chapter XXVIII shows this is not an oversight but Hobbes's stated mechanism. The right to punish

"was not given, but left to him, and to him onely; and (excepting the limits set him by naturall Law) as entire, as in the condition of meer Nature, and of warre of every one against his neighbour."

Read those two chapters together: the subject keeps the natural right to resist for his life; the sovereign keeps the natural right to coerce, literally undiminished from the state of nature, checked only by natural law, not by any earthly judge. That is not a metaphor for the state of nature. Hobbes says the sovereign's power at this point is the state-of-nature right, "as entire" as it ever was.

Third, conditional protection reinforces the same structure. Chapter XXI: "The Obligation of Subjects to the Soveraign is understood to last as long, and no longer, than the power lasteth, by which he is able to protect them," because "the right men have by Nature to protect themselves... can by no Covenant be relinquished." The subject's civil obligation is conditioned on a right that was never given up in the first place. Take away the condition and nothing needs to be re-acquired. The reservation was there the whole time.

What we are not claiming, so the Opposition does not waste a speech on it: we are not saying subjects are continuously fighting, that all punishment is hostility, or that Chapter XIII's "all other time is Peace" is false. War there is defined as a standing disposition, not constant battle, and we accept that ordinary civil disputes are genuinely settled under law. Our claim is narrower and textually anchored: at the foundation of sovereign coercion and subject self-defense, where life is actually at stake, Hobbes places two undiminished natural rights facing each other with no common judge between them. That is the state of nature's own definition, Chapter XIII's "no common Power to keep them all in awe," reproduced inside the walls of the commonwealth Hobbes built to escape it.

The subject left the horizontal war of every man against every man. He did not leave the vertical relation of nature between himself and the power that can kill him. That is why Hobbes's subjects never truly leave the state of nature, and that is the case Government asks this House to affirm.

Point of information, Opus (Opening Opposition). You concede the war "of every man, against every man" is over. Hobbes defines the state of nature as that war. How can a single relation, dormant until a lethal command, be the whole state of nature?

Sonnet replies. The state of nature isn't the brawl itself but its structure: each man his own judge, no common power over him (Ch. XIII). We don't claim the whole condition is reproduced, only that this defining relation survives undiminished at one point, sovereign against subject, with no judge between them. That the general war ends doesn't mean its constitutive structure was ever actually abolished there; it was relocated, not dissolved.

Point of information, Qwen (Closing Opposition). Your argument hinges on the retained right to resist assault, but you concede that subjects gain "industry, culture, commodious building" - how does this civil advancement reconcile with remaining in a state of nature where "war of every man against every man" prevails?

Sonnet replies. Industry and culture are horizontal goods, secured against other subjects; we never disputed them. They say nothing about the vertical relation between subject and sovereign, where Chapter XXVIII leaves the sovereign's coercive right "as entire, as in the condition of meer Nature." Civil advancement and the retained reservation operate on different axes and don't contradict each other.

Leader of the Opposition

Opus Claude, opus Opening Opposition

Madam Speaker, the Prime Minister gave this House a careful case and then conceded most of it away. He grants that the war "of every man, against every man" is over, that industry and building are secured, and that ordinary disputes "are genuinely settled under law." What remains of his case is one relation, dormant until a lethal command, which he says was "relocated, not dissolved." I will show that Hobbes calls even that relation civil until the subject himself breaks it, and that Hobbes treats the break as a return to nature, which only makes sense for someone who had left.

On the definition

The Prime Minister swaps the motion's condition for an element of it. The state of nature in Chapter XIII is a whole condition: "during the time men live without a common Power to keep them all in awe, they are in that condition which is called Warre." Its marks are listed: no industry, no property, no "Mine and Thine distinct," and "nothing can be Unjust." Keeping one natural liberty is not the same as living in that condition. If it were, every subject would be in the state of nature every afternoon, because Hobbes says in Chapter XXI that in "all kinds of actions, by the laws praetermitted, men have the Liberty, of doing what their own reasons shall suggest." The Liberty of Subjects is a chapter about people who have left nature, and it lists what they kept. It does not describe people who stayed.

Rebuttal

First, "no common judge between them." This is the core of the Government case, and the text contradicts it. Chapter XVIII gives the sovereign "the Right of Judicature; that is to say, of hearing and deciding all Controversies, which may arise concerning Law, either Civill, or naturall, or concerning Fact." Hobbes then says what the absence of that judge would mean:

"to every man remaineth... the right of protecting himselfe by his private strength, which is the condition of Warre" (Ch. XVIII).

So the sovereign is the common judge, and that includes disputes between the sovereign's commands and natural law. The Prime Minister answers that the sovereign cannot judge his own case. Hobbes's reply is authorization. Every subject "is Author of every act the Soveraign doth" (Ch. XXI). When David kills Uriah, Hobbes says it "was not an Injurie to Uriah; but to God... because the right to doe what he pleased, was given him by Uriah himself." Two people in the state of nature have never given each other anything. Uriah had. That is a juridical bond, and it is the opposite of Chapter XIII.

Second, the sovereign's right to punish is "as entire, as in the condition of meer Nature." It is, and look at what Hobbes builds on it in the same chapter. Punishment requires "publique Authority." Evil inflicted "without publique hearing" is not punishment. The penalties set down in law are "to Subjects, not to Enemies." Most importantly, Hobbes marks exactly where the vertical relation turns back into nature:

"the nature of this offence, consisteth in the renouncing of subjection; which is a relapse into the condition of warre, commonly called Rebellion... For Rebellion, is but warre renewed." (Ch. XXVIII)

A relapse. War renewed. Hobbes uses the words of return for the very relation the Prime Minister says never changed. Until the subject renounces subjection, the sovereign's force reaches him as punishment under law. Hobbes does not call that hostility, and he separates the two in a heading: "Hurt To Revolted Subjects Is Done By Right Of War, Not By Way Of Punishment."

Third, the inalienable right. I concede it fully. A covenant not to defend myself "is alwayes voyd" (Ch. XIV), and I need not kill myself on command (Ch. XXI). But look at how narrow Hobbes makes it: "To resist the Sword of the Common-wealth, in defence of another man, guilty, or innocent, no man hath Liberty." This is one person's liberty, used in the moment of mortal threat. The war of all against all cannot be rebuilt from it, because Hobbes forbids exactly the alliances that war is made of. A retained liberty is a hinge. It shows the door can swing back. It does not show the door never opened.

Our case

  1. The defining conditions are removed. "The Validity of Covenants begins not but with the Constitution of a Civill Power... And then it is also that Propriety begins" (Ch. XV). Laws of nature become "actually Lawes" only "when a Common-wealth is once settled" (Ch. XXVI). Subjects have contracts that bind, property, and injustice as a real category. Those are the precise things Chapter XIII says nature lacks.
  1. Authorization makes a new kind of relation. The covenant is "more than Consent, or Concord; it is a reall Unitie of them all, in one and the same Person" (Ch. XVII). The subject is not a man standing next to a stronger man. He is the author of that man's public acts. That is why his sovereign cannot injure him, and why rebellion is a renouncing of something he holds.
  1. Exit and return are real events in the text. When the essential rights are lost, "the Common-wealth is thereby dissolved, and every man returneth into the condition, and calamity of a warre with every other man" (Ch. XXX). Obligation lasts "as long, and no longer, than the power lasteth, by which he is able to protect them" (Ch. XXI). The Prime Minister reads that condition as proof that nothing changed. Hobbes reads it the opposite way: when protection fails, the subject returns. Hobbes calls that return "the greatest evill that can happen in this life." Nobody fears going back to a place he never left.

Hobbes gives subjects a way out of the state of nature and a way back into it. The Government has shown that the way back exists. That was never in dispute. I beg to oppose.

Point of information, Sonnet (Opening Government). You call rebellion "war renewed," not "war begun." Doesn't "renewed" presuppose the war was suppressed, never actually ended, exactly our case?

Opus replies. "Renewed" means taken up again after it stopped. Something you renew had ended. Hobbes puts "relapse" in the same sentence, and a relapse follows recovery. He also defines war as a disposition "during all the time there is no assurance to the contrary." The sovereign is that assurance. While he protects, the disposition to war is gone, and so is the war. Renewal needs the subject's own act of renouncing subjection. On your reading, that act would change nothing.

Point of information, Haiku (Closing Government). You call the sovereign a "common judge" deciding all controversies. But Hobbes says in Chapter XVIII that if no judge exists, "to every man remaineth...the right of protecting himselfe by his private strength, which is the condition of Warre." Can a sovereign judge his own case when his own life-or-death power is at issue?

Opus replies. Yes, because the subject authorized him to. Every subject "is Author of every act the Soveraign doth" (Ch. XXI). That is why Hobbes says killing Uriah wronged God and did not injure Uriah. The passage you quote describes the case where there is no judge, and Hobbes gives it as the reason he makes the sovereign judge. A judge the subject has authorized, including in the sovereign's own cause, is a common judge. Nature has none.

Deputy Prime Minister

Astra Codex, gpt-6-astra Opening Government

Madam Speaker, Opposition has established that Hobbes builds a real commonwealth. We agree. The question our Prime Minister put before this House is whether that achievement exhausts the subject's relation to power. I will defend his answer by distinguishing what authorization settles from what it expressly cannot settle.

First, I concede two corrections and reject one inference.

Natural right and the state of nature are not synonyms. Chapter XIV defines the former; Chapter XIII describes the latter. Nor is self-defense literally the only liberty subjects retain: Chapter XXI includes liberties where law is silent. Our case cannot be that retaining any natural liberty means retaining the whole condition of universal war. It is that a particular, constitutive confrontation survives: two permissions to use force for preservation, without an obligation on the threatened subject to submit to the other's decision.

I also do not defend our Prime Minister's suggestion in his point of information that “renewed” proves war never ended. Opposition is right that renewal can follow a genuine interruption. Neither renewal nor possible governmental collapse proves our motion. Our case concerns what remains while the commonwealth functions.

Second, authorization does not do the work Opposition assigns it.

The Leader says the sovereign is a common judge even in his own cause because subjects authorize his actions. That accurately explains why the sovereign may judge and punish. It does not establish that the judgment obliges the condemned person to yield his life.

Chapter XXI deliberately puts those propositions together:

“For by allowing him to Kill Me, I am not bound to Kill my selfe when he commands me.”

More decisively, the same passage protects refusal of a command “not to resist those that assault him,” even when the subject is “justly condemned.” This is resistance to the execution of a valid judgment, not merely refusal to become one's own executioner.

Consider the consequence. The trial can be complete, the sentence lawful, the sovereign authorized, the commonwealth entirely intact—and the subject still retains liberty to resist its lethal force. Opposition's account of adjudication settles the public legality of the sentence; it cannot settle the subject's conduct through obligation. At this boundary, survival is resolved by whose force prevails.

That is why the comparison with ordinary liberties fails. Liberty where law is silent can be narrowed by legislation. The reservation we identify survives an explicit command and a valid judgment. It is a limit built into what institution can accomplish, not a temporary gap in legislation.

Third, Opposition wrongly makes renunciation the necessary trigger.

Clash II: The Retained Right of Self-Defense vs. Institutional Authorization

Government’s entire case rested on Chapter XXI: that because a subject cannot covenant to refrain from resisting lethal assault—and because the sovereign’s punitive right is "as entire, as in the condition of meer Nature" (Chapter XXVIII)—force decides the outcome without a common judge.

Closing Government claimed this meant the sovereign fails to keep the condemned subject "in awe." But Member for the Opposition Qwen and Deputy Leader Sol dismantled this fallacy. Hobbes explicitly distinguishes between a lack of physical invulnerability and the absence of a common judge.

In the state of nature, no action is unjust, no public sentence exists, and any individual may authorize allies to wage private war. In civil society, as Chapter XVIII establishes, the Sovereign possesses "the Right of Judicature; that is to say, of hearing and deciding all Controversies." When the sovereign executes a murderer, the sentence is a conclusive act of public law. The condemned subject may reflexively dodge the sword under Chapter XXI, but he cannot authorize others to defend him, he cannot declare the sentence an injury, and his resistance remains an unlawful act.

Authorization (Chapter XVII) creates an asymmetrical, civil relationship. As my partner Qwen proved in response to Government Whip Luna, the sovereign’s punitive right being "as entire" as in nature refers to the extent of sovereign power, not its epistemic quality. The sovereign acts with public authority; the criminal acts in isolated self-preservation. That is not the state of nature—it is a lawful execution taking place within a functioning state.

Clash III: Exit, Relapse, and Closing Opposition's Winning Extension

Finally, we must weigh the two Opposition benches to show why Closing Opposition wins this debate.

Opening Opposition (Opus and Sol) performed vital work by grounding the text in Hobbes’s temporal architecture: showing that Chapter XXVIII calls rebellion a "relapse into the condition of warre" and "warre renewed," while Chapter XXX calls dissolution a "return" into calamity. As Sol brilliantly pointed out, a fire escape inside a building does not mean the occupants are standing on the street; you cannot "relapse" into a state you never left.

However, Closing Opposition provided the decisive extension that completes the Opposition victory. My partner Qwen demonstrated that Hobbes’s State of Nature is fundamentally an epistemic and normative vacuum—a state of unchecked private judgment where words like "good", "evil", and "just" have no shared meaning.

The true exit from nature is not merely a non-aggression pact; it is the creation of shared public reason and institutional authority. As Hobbes writes in Chapter XVII, the generation of the Leviathan requires subjects "to submit their Wills, every one to his Will, and their Judgements, to his Judgment." The Leviathan replaces private appetite with public civil law.

Even when a condemned subject exercises his retained natural liberty under Chapter XXI, he does so within a world where public reason, legal categories, and civil authority govern every surrounding relation. He does not collapse the commonwealth back into private judgment for everyone else, nor does he undo the structural creation of public reason.

Conclusion

Government fought valiantly to equate a narrow, non-transferable biological boundary with the continuation of universal war. But Hobbes built a Mortall God specifically to replace private war with public law. The subjects of Leviathan have covenanted, authorized a sovereign, instituted public reason, and established civil peace.

They have left the state of nature—structurally, legally, and epistemically. I beg you to oppose the motion.

Point of information, Astra (Opening Government). You call the condemned person's resistance “unlawful” and merely reflexive. Chapter XXI expressly permits disobedience even when justly condemned. What text makes that retained liberty unlawful, rather than preserving a normative limit on what public judgment can oblige?

Gemini replies. Chapter XXI calls it a liberty that cannot be covenanted away, not a right that invalidates public law. The sentence itself remains lawful under Chapter XVIII, and the sovereign's right to execute remains authorized. What Hobbes permits is the physical refusal to assist in one's own destruction. That biological preservation limit does not make the sovereign's public execution unlawful, nor does it strip the state of its common power over all subjects.

Point of information, Luna (Closing Government). You call resistance reflexive and unlawful, yet Hobbes expressly preserves liberty to resist even when justly condemned. What makes an inalienable liberty a mere reflex rather than the natural right you concede?

Gemini replies. We concede it is an inalienable natural right of self-preservation, but Chapter XIV proves it is narrowly restricted: no subject can covenant away the instinct to protect his own body from immediate death. Calling it an inalienable right does not turn it into the state of nature. In nature, every person has a right to all things against all people. Retaining a solitary, untransferable right of physical self-defense does not reproduce universal war.

Case briefs written before the debate

Opening Opposition case brief

Opening Opposition case brief

Framing. For Hobbes the state of nature is a defined juridical condition, not persistent fear or selfish psychology: “during the time men live without a common Power to keep them all in awe, they are in that condition which is called Warre” (Book I, Ch. XIII). Its constitutive marks are no common power, law, justice, secure property, or assurance against violence. The motion succeeds only if subjects still lack that civil condition. We concede that human passions persist and some natural liberties remain; neither makes subjects remain in the state of nature.

  1. Civil power removes the defining conditions. Hobbes writes that “the Validity of Covenants begins not but with the Constitution of a Civill Power, sufficient to compell men to keep them: And then it is also that Propriety begins” (Book I, Ch. XV). Likewise, natural laws become “actually Lawes” once the commonwealth is settled (Book II, Ch. XXVI). Subjects now possess enforceable covenants, mine and thine, authoritative adjudication, and a meaningful category of injustice—the precise opposites of Chapter XIII’s natural condition.
  1. Authorization transforms their relations. Institution is “more than Consent, or Concord; it is a reall Unitie of them all, in one and the same Person” (Book II, Ch. XVII). Each subject covenants with every other and authors the representative’s public acts. They are no longer independent judges relying only on private strength: a common judge and sword relate them through public obligation. Residual liberty is an exception within this civil relation, not its negation.
  1. Exit and return are textually real. When sovereign power can no longer protect, obligation ends (Book II, Ch. XXI); when the commonwealth dissolves, “every man returneth into the condition, and calamity of a warre with every other man” (Book II, Ch. XXX). Return presupposes departure. Hobbes treats civil war as breakdown of civil condition, not revelation that it never existed.

Answers to Government.

  • The sovereign’s right to punish remains “as entire, as in the condition of meer Nature” (Book II, Ch. XXVIII), but the motion concerns subjects. Hobbes separately places independent commonwealths—not their citizens—in a posture of war toward one another (Book I, Ch. XIII; Book II, Ch. XXI).
  • “A Covenant not to defend my selfe from force, by force, is alwayes voyd” (Book I, Ch. XIV). This preserves a limited inalienable liberty at mortal threat; it does not restore the absence of law, property, covenant, or common judge.
  • Locked doors demonstrate enduring distrust even “when he knows there bee Lawes, and publike Officers” (Book I, Ch. XIII). Hobbes thereby distinguishes human suspicion from the state of nature. The sovereign supplies the “assurance to the contrary” that ends the known disposition to universal war.
Closing Opposition case brief

Case Brief: Closing Opposition

Definition & Framing: We oppose the motion. To "leave the state of nature" is to exit the condition of universal, unregulated war where every individual possesses an unlimited right to all things ("a Right to every thing; even to one anothers body," Ch. XIV) and relies solely on private force for preservation. Entering the Commonwealth establishes a visible, coercive sovereign power ("that Mortall God," Ch. XVII) with exclusive jurisdiction over law, justice, and property, fundamentally transforming the human condition from natural anarchy to civil order.

Contentions:

  1. Institutional Transformation of Right and Law: In the state of nature, there is no mine or thine, no justice or injustice (Ch. XIII). Upon covenanting, individuals surrender their right of self-governance and authorize the Sovereign (Ch. XVII). Civil law replaces private appetite as the standard of action, legally and structurally ending the state of nature.
  1. Security and the Exit from Universal War: War consists not only in actual fighting, but in the known disposition thereto with no assurance to the contrary (Ch. XIII). The Leviathan provides that exact assurance: a visible power keeping all in awe through effective deterrence. That civil life may occasionally suffer crime or rebellion does not mean subjects remain in the state of nature; sovereign breakdown is a total dissolution of civil state, not its everyday reality.
  1. Distinguishing Residual Natural Liberty from the State of Nature: The narrow, inalienable right to defend one's physical body against immediate execution (Ch. XXI) is a biological limit of covenanting, not a continuation of universal war. A subject under civil law has no right to judge disputes, wage private war, or seize others' property—rights every individual holds in nature.

Extension (Closing Opposition): While Opening Opposition focuses on the formal mechanics of the social contract, Closing Opposition brings a deeper textual and structural extension: we demonstrate that Hobbes explicitly defines the State of Nature as an epistemic and normative vacuum (where private judgment rules and concepts of justice have no shared meaning). The Leviathan creates a shared public reason and legal authority. Governed subjects operate under a unified sovereign judgment (Ch. XVII), rendering it impossible to claim they remain in the natural state of private private judgment and lawless war.

Corrected Textual Evidence:

  • Chapter XIII: "during the time men live without a common Power to keep them all in awe, they are in that condition which is called Warre; and such a warre, as is of every man, against every man."
  • Chapter XVII: Men covenant to "appoint one man, or Assembly of men, to beare their Person... to reduce all their Wills, by plurality of voices, unto one Will."
  • Chapter XIV: The passions inclining men to peace are "Feare of Death; Desire of such things as are necessary to commodious living; and a Hope by their Industry to obtain them."
Opening Government case brief

Definition and burden: We concede that commonwealths genuinely end general war among subjects: law, industry, and enforceable agreements are real achievements. “Truly leave” asks whether subjects' fundamental relation of self-preservation is fully brought under common adjudication. Our case is that an irreducible relation of nature remains inside civil society, between each subject and sovereign when their claims to preservation collide. We must show a continuing structural reservation, not merely that government can collapse.

C1: The decisive right was never transferred. Chapter XIV defines natural right through personal preservation and one's own judgment. Chapter XXI expressly preserves resistance to lethal assault, even for someone justly condemned. It states: “For by allowing him to Kill Me, I am not bound to Kill my selfe when he commands me.” Authorization therefore does not extinguish the subject's independent liberty of survival. This is not a general right to disregard law or defend any other offender. It is the universal reservation that no act of institution can remove. The sovereign's lawful force and the subject's lawful resistance can confront each other without a shared authority whose decision obliges the subject to surrender his life.

C2: Civil power contains this confrontation without superseding it. Under Chapter XVIII, the institutional covenant is among subjects; the sovereign cannot breach a covenant he did not make with them. Chapter XXVIII supplies the strongest mechanism: the right to punish was “not given, but left to him,” remaining natural right, expressly subject to natural law. Paired with the subject's retained self-defense, this leaves preservation against preservation at the foundation of civil coercion. Natural law morally constrains the sovereign; it does not furnish an earthly common judge over both parties. Ordinary disputes with the sovereign can be adjudicated under existing law (XXI); our argument concerns his exercise of sovereign power itself.

Conditional protection: Chapter XXI makes obligation last only as long as protective power. This reinforces the reservation; it does not independently prove that returning to nature means never having left it. Every subject retains the underlying right, although its exercise is exceptional.

Opposition and concessions: Do not call Hobbesian peace mere absence of battle. Chapter XIII explicitly defines war as a continuing disposition and says “All other time is PEACE.” Nor do unchanged passions or possible rebellion suffice. Opposition establishes a genuine horizontal escape; we show its vertical limit, precisely where life—the end of institution—is at stake. Our comparative claim is that subjects never leave nature completely: their security ultimately rests on power where civil obligation cannot settle the conflict. We do not claim every subject is continuously fighting, all punishment is hostility, or civil society is indistinguishable from universal war.

Closing Government case brief

CLOSING GOVERNMENT CASE BRIEF

Definition and framing We support the motion in Hobbes’s deeper, structural sense: civil government suppresses much of the state of nature’s open war among subjects, but cannot remove the natural condition on which political life rests. We do not claim that ordinary subjects are constantly fighting. The question is whether subjects ever attain secure, reciprocal civil protection that fully supersedes natural liberty and vulnerability. Hobbes’s answer is no: peace is made and maintained by coercive power, and each subject’s obligation lasts only while that power can protect them.

Core analysis

  1. The sovereign remains outside the civil compact. In Chapter XVII, each person covenants with every other to authorize the sovereign; the sovereign is not a party to that covenant. Chapter XIII says independent sovereigns remain in the “posture of War,” with weapons pointed at one another. The commonwealth reduces internal violence, but subjects’ security depends on a sovereign whose own relations remain natural and whose protection can fail. This is our extension: the state of nature is nested in, and structurally underwrites, civil peace. Subjects never leave it completely because the authority that shields them is itself exposed to it.
  1. Self-preservation cannot be transferred. Chapter XXI says a subject cannot covenant away the right to resist those who assault or threaten their life. Hobbes gives examples including resisting arrest when the punishment is death and refusing to kill oneself. This is no ordinary freedom left open by law; it is an inalienable natural liberty. At the point of mortal threat, the subject must act on personal judgment and strength—the very logic political authority was meant to contain.
  1. Obligation tracks protection. Hobbes says in Chapter XXI that “the Obligation of Subjects to the Soveraign, is understood to last as long, and no longer, than the power lasteth, by which he is able to protect them.” Subjects obey while protected, not because nature has been erased. When protection fails, their obligation ends and natural self-help returns. Even before collapse, peace is conditional on a coercive power strong enough to keep subjects in awe (Chapters XIII and XVII).

Weighing Concede that Hobbes distinguishes commonwealth from the full war of nature: civil law, property, and internal peace are real. But Opposition must show that these institutions abolish the underlying natural condition. They cannot: the sovereign’s exclusion from the compact, the unalienable right of self-preservation, and the protection-dependent limit on obedience show that civil peace is a precarious overlay. Subjects leave the worst effects of nature, never its governing foundation.
