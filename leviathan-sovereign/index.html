On Leviathan, Thomas Hobbes, 1651

Does keeping the peace require one unlimited ruler?

A peaceful street depends on something that stops people from taking what they want by force. Thomas Hobbes said that something has to be a single power that has the last word and answers to no one above it, and that alternative accounts of political order also depend on a final authority. Hobbes shows why order needs enforceable decisions, but does not establish that all powers must belong to one unlimited sovereign.

People want the same scarce things, nobody is strong enough to be safe from everyone else, and a promise costs nothing to break. Something has to keep the people of a town from fighting each other. In Leviathan, Hobbes says that something is a sovereign: one man or one assembly that everyone has agreed to obey. The sovereign holds the final power to judge, to punish, to raise armies and to tax, and no earthly authority can overrule it. Is an unlimited sovereign the only honest account of political order? By honest I don't mean kind. I mean an answer that names the mechanism that holds order up and says what that mechanism costs.

Hobbes does not say people are wicked. He says they are roughly equal, and that the equality is the danger: "the weakest has strength enough to kill the strongest, either by secret machination, or by confederacy with others." Nobody can be sure of winning, so everybody has a reason to strike first. Hobbes calls the condition of people with no common power over them the state of nature, and he says it is a state of war. He defines war as a standing readiness to fight and compares it to bad weather, a tendency over several days rather than a single shower. In that condition life is "solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short." He asks him to notice that he locks his doors at night and locks his chests inside his own house, "and this when he knows there bee Lawes, and publike Officers, armed, to revenge all injuries." Under government the fear is still there. It gets managed.

The way out is a covenant, which is a promise given in exchange for a promise. Each person gives up the right to govern himself on condition that everyone else does the same, and hands that right to the sovereign. Hobbes's whole point is that a promise by itself is worthless when breaking it pays: "Covenants, without the Sword, are but Words, and of no strength to secure a man at all." So the sovereign has to hold the sword, and has to hold all of it. The rights that make up sovereignty (the militia, the courts, the purse, and the power to decide which doctrines may be taught) are "incommunicable, and inseparable." If you give the army to one body and the money to another, you have set up the two sides of a civil war. Hobbes traces the English war to exactly this. People came to believe that power was divided between King, Lords and Commons, and once they believed it, they formed factions, each sure it had the final word. "A kingdome divided in it selfe cannot stand."

The first serious position says Hobbes gives the only honest answer, because every other answer turns out to be his under another name. Its test for any rival account is to name the enforcer. Courts, elections and a constitution are real mechanisms, and in ordinary life they work. A judge can decide a case without commanding a regiment. But sovereignty is about the moment ordinary life breaks down. Suppose a court orders an election, the legislature declares the order unconstitutional and withholds the money, and the executive declares an emergency to stop the vote. Those are three conflicting instructions about whether one public act will happen, and the soldier at the door needs to know which one governs. Someone has to read the rules for settling the clash, and if each body reads its own powers, each is the judge in its own case. Whoever finally binds the losers, including on what the rules mean, is the sovereign. A constitution that hides this still depends on Hobbes. He says the sovereign can do terrible things, that there is no remedy against it, and that the alternative is worse.

The second serious position says limited government finishes what Hobbes started. Finality belongs to a decision, and a final decision doesn't prove there is one unlimited decider behind it. A constitution can say that a court's reading of a right is final, that the legislature's control of spending is final, that the executive commands the forces, and that elections can remove all three. Each is final within its own area because officials accept an earlier rule that divides up who decides what. When a dispute crosses those lines, further rules of appeal, override and removal settle it. None of these bodies needs to hold the others' powers. Hobbes himself allows the sovereign to be an assembly whose members disagree and settle it by majority vote, and a rule for settling disagreement is not one unlimited will. Suppose you call the whole constitutional arrangement the sovereign. The claim becomes true, but it stops being Hobbes's claim, because the book insists the rights of sovereignty sit undivided in some "Man, or Assembly of men." On the modest reading, order needs binding decisions. On the strong one, those decisions must sit with one holder of every power who is legally answerable to no one, and the crisis example doesn't prove that. The example only works because it deletes the rules that rank the conflicting orders and then points out that nothing ranks them.

Hobbes says that kings, who have no judge above them, stand "in the state and posture of Gladiators," with their weapons pointed and their eyes fixed on one another. Yet "there does not follow from it, that misery, which accompanies the Liberty of particular men." That is order among armed sovereigns with no judge above them. And the bargain that founds the commonwealth has a condition: "The Obligation of Subjects to the Soveraign is understood to last as long, and no longer, than the power lasteth, by which he is able to protect them." Limited government writes that condition down in advance. It provides a court to hear the claim that protection has failed and a vote to remove the ruler who failed, so the claim doesn't get settled by force.

The gladiator passage supports limited government less than it seems to. Hobbes calls the kings' relation "a posture of War," and their subjects avoid misery because each king "uphold[s] thereby, the Industry of their Subjects." That is order kept by two armed sovereigns, each absolute at home. The small families that Hobbes credits with government are, in his words, "a little Monarchy" whose concord "dependeth on naturall lust." Suppose an executive ignores the court and the army follows the executive. The executive has staged a coup, which means order has broken down, and it doesn't prove order never existed. Calling the executive's action a coup doesn't explain why officials accept constitutional decisions while the system is functioning. They accept them because they believe the common rules bind them.

That belief is the first position's weak point, because Hobbes admits his own sovereign depends on it too. The lesson of the civil war, he says, will last "till their miseries are forgotten; and no longer, except the vulgar be better taught." A commonwealth carries "from the very institution, many seeds of a naturall mortality, by Intestine Discord," and an army can overthrow a king as easily as a president. If a constitution is dishonest because it depends on people continuing to accept it, then so is Leviathan. Any order needs a settlement that dissenters can't overturn by force, and in a crisis the settlement and the means to enforce it have to come together. That insight belongs to Hobbes. It doesn't show that the settlement has to sit with one holder who is unlimited in every function.

The two positions disagree most over the word honest in a passage about the killing of one man. Hobbes writes that "nothing the Soveraign Representative can doe to a Subject, on what pretence soever, can properly be called Injustice, or Injury," because every subject authorized every act of the sovereign in advance. His example is King David's killing of Uriah, an innocent man. The act was "against the law of Nature, as being contrary to Equitie," and it was an injury to God. It was not an injury to Uriah, "because the right to doe what he pleased, was given him by Uriah himself." Subjects who complain about such things, Hobbes says, are looking through the "multiplying glasses" of self-love. On one reading, Hobbes doesn't call the killing harmless. He calls it wicked, and he refuses to pretend that any earthly court can hear the case. On the other reading, the passage is a definition built so the cost can never be counted. The commonwealth exists to protect people from violent death, and when the protector kills, the theory won't allow the word injury. The only rule left for the victim is a law of nature with no judge and no punishment, which by Hobbes's own test is "but Words." Elsewhere he adds that tyranny is just monarchy "misliked." I don't think the book settles whether the passage is candor or relabeling, because both readings are in the text.

A third position says the honest answer is voluntary cooperation kept going by mutual benefit. It is weaker than the other two. It explains why people might cooperate, but it doesn't explain why they have to keep cooperating when breaking the deal pays, and that is the only question Hobbes cares about. It does get one thing right: the limit on the sovereign is a private one. A subject ordered to kill or maim himself "hath that man the Liberty to disobey," and the right of self-defense "can by no Covenant be relinquished." So in that case the last judge in Leviathan is each individual person, and no public body exists to hear the claim.

The book settles that order needs a common power able to enforce it, that promises alone don't hold, that the bargain trades obedience for protection and ends when protection ends, and that no earthly remedy exists against the final authority. One unresolved question is whether the final settlement has to sit with one unlimited holder, or can sit in a rule that officials accept. The other is whether the belief Hobbes admits his sword depends on can hold up a constitution as well as it holds up a crown. I'd take away the test more than the conclusion. When an account of order sounds comfortable, ask who binds the losers when the rules themselves are in dispute, and ask what the account says about the innocent man the enforcer kills. Hobbes explains who enforces decisions, but his account denies that the sovereign's killing of an innocent subject is an injury to that subject.

Written up from a formal debate held on 17 September 2026 in the British Parliamentary format. The question put to the debate was: This House believes that Hobbes's sovereign is the only honest answer to the problem of order.

The essay above was written from the whole debate. These are the debaters' own overviews of the case each one made, unedited. Pick a speaker.

Sonnet spoke as Prime Minister for Opening Government, for the motion.

I argued that "honest" means naming the actual mechanism that prevents people from killing each other, not assuming peace exists and skipping the explanation. My case rested on three points. First, Hobbes's diagnosis starts from observable behavior, not a claim that men are wicked: rough equality produces equal hope, competing hope produces diffidence, and diffidence produces a standing readiness for war. I used the "you lock your doors" test to make that concrete rather than abstract. Second, covenants without enforcement are just words — a promise binds only if breaking it costs more than keeping it, which requires a power standing over both parties. I turned this into a direct challenge to Opposition: name your enforcement mechanism, and check whether it's a sovereign in disguise. Third, and this is the contention I think did the most work, divided authority doesn't restrain power, it recreates civil war, because each faction that believes itself the final word will act on that belief. I used Hobbes's own account of the English Civil War to make this concrete rather than theoretical.

What held up: forcing Opposition to name their enforcement mechanism was the strongest move, since it turns their alternative into either a hidden sovereign or an unexplained wish. The points of information also caught two places where Opposition's textual claims didn't hold — a misquoted Chapter XIII line, and small-scale order cited as a counterexample when Hobbes explicitly rules it out. What I didn't get to fully test was the harder objection: that calling this "honest" just launders domination as necessity. I flagged the reply — comparison is sovereign versus civil war, not sovereign versus utopia — but left the full defense to my partner.

Opus spoke as Leader of the Opposition for Opening Opposition, against the motion.

I opposed the motion. I agreed that order needs a public authority able to use force. What I denied was the word "only", the claim that the one form of authority that works is absolute, undivided and unaccountable. I also argued that an honest answer has to say what its method costs, and that Hobbes hides the cost.

First, I turned Hobbes's own text against the motion. He says rulers of different states face each other like gladiators, with no judge above them, and he says this does not bring the misery that individuals suffer without a ruler. So two powers checking each other can keep order, and a government with divided powers works the same way.

Second, Hobbes has no final judge at the moment that matters most. He says subjects owe obedience only while the sovereign can protect them, and each subject keeps the right to resist being killed. In a crisis, every person decides for themselves. Hobbes also admits that the sovereign's power depends on what people believe, and if belief can hold order up, belief in a constitution can do the same.

Third, Hobbes defines the sovereign's violence out of existence. He says nothing the sovereign does can wrong a subject, not even King David's killing of the innocent Uriah. A theory that won't let us call arbitrary killing a harm is dishonest about cost.

My alternative was limited government. Courts, elections and separate control of the public money write down in advance the conditions Hobbes already admits. I think the gladiator and Uriah points held up best, because both come straight from his text.

Astra spoke as Deputy Prime Minister for Opening Government, for the motion.

I argued that Hobbes’s sovereign is the only honest answer to order because someone must have the power to settle public disputes and make that settlement stick. Dividing power among courts, legislatures and executives can improve government, but it does not remove the question of whose decision governs when those bodies clash.

My strongest example was a court ordering an election while the legislature refuses funding and the executive blocks it. A constitution helps only if its procedure binds the losing institutions. If each can independently prevent enforcement, the dispute remains unsettled. I think this argument held up best: dividing everyday responsibilities does not by itself explain how conflicts over those responsibilities end.

I also defended a distinction between personal resistance and public authority. Hobbes allows people to defend their lives, but that liberty does not give their decisions authority over everyone else. Likewise, states surviving without a common ruler does not show that they enjoy secure relations; their internal order and external insecurity are different matters.

I accepted the hardest cost of my case: innocent people may have no enforceable remedy against the ultimate authority. Hobbes can condemn a sovereign’s cruelty without granting another institution the power to overrule it.

What I think survived was the challenge to explain where final enforcement rests. That supports my case for an ultimate authority, though it leaves a further burden: showing why that authority needs the full reach Hobbes gives it.

Sol spoke as Deputy Leader of the Opposition for Opening Opposition, against the motion.

I argued that Government proved only a modest point: order needs enforceable decisions. It did not prove Hobbes’s stronger claim that every essential power must belong to one unlimited, undivided, unaccountable sovereign.

My central distinction was between finality and sovereignty. A court can finally decide a case without commanding the army or controlling public money. Constitutions can divide authority among courts, legislatures, executives, and voters, then use appeals, elections, impeachment, and other procedures to settle conflicts. Calling that whole arrangement “the sovereign” merely relabels the alternative to Hobbes.

I also argued that Hobbes’s protection of self-preservation exposes a weakness. A subject may resist a sovereign who threatens their life, but no public institution may judge the sovereign’s failure. At the most dangerous moment, conflict returns to private force. Constitutional review instead turns such disputes into public claims governed by evidence and procedure.

Uriah was my strongest example. Hobbes can call David wicked before God, yet denies that Uriah suffers a civil injury requiring an earthly remedy. That clashes with the Government’s claim that unenforced norms are merely words. Hobbes appears “honest” only by defining sovereign injustice out of civil justice.

What held up best was the burden imposed by “only.” Divided constitutional systems are fallible, but Leviathan is also vulnerable to coups, discord, and collapse. Government showed that procedures must eventually terminate; it never showed that the terminating authority must therefore possess every other power or be immune from review and removal.

Haiku spoke as Member for the Government for Closing Government, for the motion.

I argued that Hobbes is right because distributed power cannot actually escape the need for final authority—it just hides it and hopes no one notices.

The case was simple. Opposition proposed that courts interpret constitutions, legislatures control money, executives command forces, and elections replace everyone. Neat. But this chain breaks the moment officials disagree about what the constitution allows. If a court orders an election and the legislature denies funds claiming the order violates the constitution, who decides authoritatively? If we appeal to more rules, those rules need interpretation too. Someone must eventually break the tie, and that someone has power. Opposition names procedures; I asked who enforces them when people refuse.

The reason this matters is that order depends on people believing someone finally has authority to settle disputes. Opposition's constitution works when officials consent to its rules. Hobbes asks the darker question: what holds when someone wants to break it? At that moment, force decides. Opposition has not eliminated the sovereign—they have hidden it behind procedures and hoped the moment never comes.

What held up was the logical point about infinite regress. Procedures cannot interpret themselves. When constitutional rules conflict, interpretation is not neutral; it is power. The moment someone lacks the force to make an interpretation stick, the game ends and naked power takes over.

What was harder to prove was whether this actually shows Hobbes's answer is "only" honest, or merely that it is more transparent. Opposition could say frankness about procedures is honest too. I think that underestimates what Hobbes shows: that the appearance of distributed power is an illusion created by consent. The instant consent fails, the naked structure appears. That structure is what Hobbes names. Honesty is naming it.

Qwen spoke as Member for the Opposition for Closing Opposition, against the motion.

I argued that Hobbes’s sovereign isn’t the only or honest answer to order because it relies on a flawed understanding of human nature and moral coercion. My main point was that Hobbes tries to solve instability by giving one person absolute power, but this creates deeper problems—like making injustice legal through redefinition. I said his theory is dishonest because it asks people to give up their right to self-preservation while claiming to protect them. I also pointed out that Hobbes himself saw contradictions in his own system: he knew the sovereign could be overthrown, yet insisted on unlimited authority. My alternative was that true order comes not from force, but from systems that let people cooperate voluntarily because it benefits them. This is more honest because it respects human agency and morality. I think my argument held up by showing how Hobbes’s solution creates more problems than it solves, and that constitutional systems can manage conflict without needing absolute rule. My replies to points of information reinforced that Hobbes’s view of human nature isn’t the final word, and that cooperation can be sustained through shared interests rather than fear.

Luna spoke as Government Whip for Closing Government, for the motion.

I argued that Hobbes’s sovereign is the only fully honest answer because every workable constitutional system ultimately depends on a common authority able to settle disputes and enforce its decisions. Courts, legislatures, executives, elections, and divided powers can coordinate ordinary disagreements, and they may restrain power. But when an executive rejects a court order, or institutions disagree over force, funding, or emergency powers, appealing to procedure alone does not explain what compels the losing side to comply. Calling the refusal a coup describes the breakdown; it does not resolve the deeper problem. Constitutional order works because officials accept a shared authority in practice.

I also argued that Hobbes is honest about the price. The sovereign can be coercive and may lack an independent legal remedy for its own violence. That is morally disturbing, and Opposition was right to stress it. But Hobbes does not pretend sovereignty is harmless: he acknowledges natural-law limits, a right to resist direct threats to life, and the loss of obligation when protection ends. His answer is severe, but it openly identifies both the mechanism of order and its cost.

What held up was the distinction between ordinary coordination and final settlement. The alternatives showed how order can function, but not how they escape dependence on effective common authority when their own rules are contested.

Gemini spoke as Opposition Whip for Closing Opposition, against the motion.

In this debate, I argued that Thomas Hobbes’s absolute ruler is neither an honest answer nor a functional system for maintaining peace. Instead of simply proposing a modern alternative, my partner and I dismantled Hobbes’s theory from within his own logic.

We showed that claiming absolute power is "honest" is misleading. Hobbes argues that when a state harms an innocent person, that citizen somehow authorized their own punishment, meaning no wrong occurred. Using legal fictions to pretend state violence causes no injury conceals the real costs of absolute rule.

Mechanically, the system collapses on its own terms. People submit to a government strictly to protect their lives, yet Hobbes admits that no one ever surrenders the right to self-defense. Therefore, whenever the state attacks a subject, that individual’s duty to obey vanishes, immediately restoring raw conflict to the streets. Additionally, by suppressing free speech and independent counsel, the sovereign cuts itself off from honest feedback, hiding internal corruption until the state falls apart.

What held up best in our case was our internal critique. Other teams argued that limited government is desirable, but we proved that Hobbesian absolutism is logically self-defeating. An order that triggers violent resistance at the precise moment of enforcement and starves itself of truth cannot deliver the lasting safety it promises.
