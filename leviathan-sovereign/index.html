A debate on Leviathan, Thomas Hobbes (1651)

This House believes that Hobbes's sovereign is the only honest answer to the problem of order

Thomas Hobbes wrote Leviathan in 1651, during the English Civil War, a war inside England over who held power. His problem was simple. People are roughly equal, they want the same scarce things, and they can't trust each other. With nobody to keep them in line, they live in fear. His answer was that everyone should hand their power to one ruler, or one ruling group. That ruler's decisions are final, and nobody can challenge them in court. This debate asked if that answer is honest. One side said an honest answer names what holds order up, which is one final power with a sword. On that view Hobbes is honest, because he names it and admits what it costs. The other side said an honest answer also has to count what the ruler does to the people. They said Hobbes builds a theory where the ruler's violence can never be called an injury. Below all of this was a deeper disagreement. Say a constitution has courts, elections and a separate purse (control of the money). Is that a different kind of order, or the same sovereign wearing a different hat?

In brief

The debate asked if Hobbes's all-powerful ruler is the only honest way to explain what stops people from fighting each other. One side said yes. They argued that any order that works needs one final power nobody can overrule, and that constitutions (the written rules that set up a government) just hide that power. The other side agreed that order needs a public power with a sword. But they said rules can split that power up, other offices can check it, and people can remove it. They also said Hobbes's own book shows order without a single boss. A third argument said Hobbes's ruler defeats itself, because subjects keep the right to fight back when the ruler attacks them. By the end, both sides agreed on two things: order needs enforcement, and Hobbes leaves innocent victims of the ruler with no way to get justice. One question stayed open. When a rule decides which decision wins, is that a hidden sovereign (a ruler with the final say), or a real alternative?

The positions

One final sword: every order ends in an authority nobody can overrule

If an explanation of order doesn't end in one final power that can't be split and can enforce its decisions, then it's secretly relying on that kind of power, or it hasn't solved the problem.

Why people hold it

  • People lock their doors even where there are laws and police, so fear doesn't go away by itself. An honest theory has to explain that instinct instead of assuming peace.
  • A promise with nobody to enforce it is just words. Whatever you propose instead, someone has to make breaking the deal cost more than keeping it, and that someone is a sovereign under a different name.
  • Splitting up the sword, the purse and the courts causes civil war instead of curing it. Hobbes blames the English war on the belief that power was split between the King, the Lords and the Commons (the two houses of Parliament).
  • Take a crisis. A court orders an election, the legislature cuts off the money, and the executive declares an emergency. Any rules for settling that clash have to be interpreted by someone, so you can't dodge the question of who decides last.
  • Being honest means naming how order works and what it costs. Hobbes admits you have no legal way to fight the final power, and he doesn't pretend the ruler is harmless.

Where it held up

  • The text backs up the main reading. Chapter XVIII says the core rights of a sovereign can't be separated. If the ruler gives away the army, the courts are useless. If the ruler gives away taxes, the army is useless.
  • The other side didn't fully answer the crisis example. They said a coup is a breakdown and doesn't prove the system never existed. That describes the failure, but it doesn't say what forces the losing branch of government to obey.
  • This side read the gladiators passage correctly. Hobbes does say rulers facing each other are in a posture of war. He also says their subjects avoid misery only because each ruler keeps order at home.
  • This side gave up ground where the text required it. They admitted that fear survives inside the state, that constitutional procedures work, and that the innocent man Uriah is wronged.

Where it fell short

  • The things they gave up made the claim smaller. By the end, they were defending the idea that any order needs decisions that officials treat as binding. That's true, but plenty of thinkers besides Hobbes would say it, and the motion said "only".
  • They said constitutions "hide" the sovereign. The reply was that calling the whole setup a sovereign just wins by changing the label. This side didn't show why a rule that ranks decisions has to collapse into one person or group holding all the power.
  • The claim that Hobbes "does not flinch from calling it tyrannical" isn't in the book. Chapter XIX says tyranny is just the name unhappy people give to monarchy (rule by a king or queen).
  • Their defense on Uriah relied on a moral wrong that nobody on earth enforces. By their own test, a rule nobody enforces is just words. The other side pointed out this problem, and it was never solved.

Limited government as the honest completion of Hobbes

Order needs a public power with a sword. But rules can decide who holds that power, other offices can check it, and a set process can remove it. Hobbes's own text includes the conditional deal that makes this the honest version of his idea.

Why people hold it

  • Hobbes himself says you only owe obedience as long as the ruler can protect you. So the deal has conditions. Limited government writes those conditions down ahead of time, so each person doesn't have to judge them alone in the street.
  • A decision being final doesn't prove there's one unlimited decider behind it. A constitution can say which court has the last word on which question, and how a legislature can overrule it. When Hobbes's own ruling assembly disagrees, it settles things by majority vote, and that's a rule, not one person's unlimited will.
  • Hobbes's rulers face each other with no judge above them, yet their people aren't miserable. That's order between powers with nobody on top, at the most dangerous level there is.
  • Hobbes says the whole lesson of the civil war rests on opinion and will only last until people forget their suffering. If order rests on what people believe, then belief in a constitution can hold it up just as well as belief in a king.
  • Hobbes says nothing the ruler does to a subject counts as injustice, and that Uriah gave permission for his own killing. That's a definition built so the cost can never be counted, and calling it "naming the cost" is wrong.

Where it held up

  • The quotations this side relied on all check out against the text. That includes the conditional duty to obey, the freedom to refuse an order to harm yourself, the Uriah passage, the multiplying glasses (Hobbes's image of lenses that make a small burden look huge), and the "till their miseries are forgotten" line.
  • The Uriah point made the other side admit a plain truth: an innocent man is wronged, and Hobbes gives him no way to get justice. The other side didn't take that back.
  • The "relabelling" argument held up. If any final settlement counts as a sovereign, the motion is true by definition and stops being about Hobbes.
  • This side pointed out that in a crisis, Hobbes's system leaves the final judgment to each subject. The other side admitted this was an "irreducible conflict", meaning one that can't be solved.

Where it fell short

  • The family example was weak. Hobbes calls a large independent family a little monarchy with the father as sovereign, and he says small groups don't give people security. The text doesn't use families as an example of balanced powers.
  • The gladiators passage proves less than this side claimed. Hobbes calls that relationship a posture of war. He says the peace comes from each ruler's power at home, not from the rulers checking each other.
  • This side answered the crisis question with a list of mechanisms: chains of command, funding and loyalty to the constitution. Then they said total failure is a coup. That never says what enforces the process when the one holding the sword rejects it.
  • This side said small families are held together by "natural affection". That softens the text, which says their peace depends on "naturall lust", meaning natural desire.

The sovereign defeats itself: order should rest on cooperation, not fear

Hobbes's ruler fails by Hobbes's own standards, for two reasons. Once the ruler attacks a subject, that subject's right to protect themselves brings back war. And a ruler who controls all teaching shuts off the honest warnings it needs to survive.

Why people hold it

  • Hobbes says that no covenant (a binding agreement) can take away your right to protect yourself when nobody else will. So right when the ruler uses force on a person, that person's duty to obey disappears, and the fighting moves back to the street.
  • Hobbes says the commonwealth carries the seeds of its own death through fighting inside it. A ruler who decides which ideas may be taught silences the criticism that would warn of that decay.
  • Order lasts because people cooperate when it benefits them, and constant terror doesn't produce that. Constitutional systems set things up so cooperating pays better than breaking the deal.

Where it held up

  • The self-protection point is solid in the text. Chapter XXI does keep the freedom to refuse an order to kill, wound or maim yourself. Chapter XVIII does give the ruler the power to judge which ideas are fit to be taught.
  • The "coherence" test for honesty says a theory is honest if its results match what it says it's for. That's a different standard from "naming how order works", and the other side never argued against it directly.

Where it fell short

  • Two passages this side gave as Hobbes's words aren't in the book. When someone challenged one of them, the reply said the quotation was accurate. It wasn't.
  • This side didn't answer the main question about enforcement. People helping each other for mutual benefit explains why they might cooperate. It doesn't explain why they have to keep cooperating when breaking the deal pays better, and that moment is what Hobbes's whole argument is about.
  • This side read the claim that the sovereign is "subject to violent death" as meaning the ruler can be overthrown by force but not by law. The passage is about the commonwealth dying from war with other countries or fighting inside it. It doesn't say what the debate used it to say.
  • The censorship argument only came up in the final speech. It cited Chapter XXIX for a disease called "lack of counsel" (missing good advice), but that chapter doesn't list it. The other side fairly asked where this had been shown earlier.

Key ideas, explained

State of nature

This is Hobbes's picture of life when there's no shared power for everyone to fear. People are roughly equal, so even the weakest person can kill the strongest by a trick or by teaming up with others. Nobody can trust anyone, and life is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short". Hobbes compares it to bad weather: people aren't fighting all the time, but they're always ready to.

Covenant

A covenant is a promise where one side does its part now and trusts the other side to do theirs later. Hobbes says these promises are worthless unless someone can punish the one who breaks them: "Covenants, without the Sword, are but Words". The debate kept coming back to this line.

Sovereign

The sovereign is the person or group everyone agrees to obey so that promises can be enforced. Hobbes is clear it can be one man or an assembly. Its decisions are final, and subjects can't challenge it in court. Its core powers (the army, taxes, courts, and control over what gets taught) can't be split off without making the rest useless.

Inseparable rights of sovereignty

In Chapter XVIII, Hobbes argues that the core powers have to stay together. If the ruler gives away the army, the courts can't enforce anything. If the ruler gives away taxes, nobody can pay the army. Hobbes blames the English Civil War on the belief that these powers were split between the King, the Lords and the Commons.

Liberty of subjects

Hobbes lists a few things a subject can refuse even to a sovereign. You may resist anyone who comes to kill you. You may refuse an order to kill, wound or starve yourself. And you only owe obedience as long as the ruler can protect you. The debate hinged on whether these limits are an honest boundary or a hole in the theory.

No injury from the sovereign

Subjects gave the ruler permission for everything it does, so Hobbes says the ruler can never treat them unjustly. His example is King David having the innocent Uriah killed. Hobbes says this goes against the law of nature and wrongs God, but it isn't an injury to Uriah, because Uriah had given David the right to do as he pleased.

The gladiators passage

Hobbes says kings facing each other, with no judge above them, stand like gladiators with their weapons pointed at each other. He calls this a posture of war. But their people don't suffer the misery of the state of nature, because each king keeps order at home. One side read this as order with no authority on top. The other side read it as two sovereigns, which is different from having none.

Tyranny as monarchy misliked

In Chapter XIX, Hobbes says tyranny isn't a separate kind of government. It's just the name people give to monarchy when they're unhappy living under it. This matters because it shows Hobbes doesn't accept the cost of his ruler and call it "tyranny". He rejects the word.

Where the sides agreed

Both sides accepted more than the motion (the statement up for debate) suggests.

  • Order needs a public power with a sword. Neither side argued that goodwill alone keeps order. The side arguing for limited government named, as its own answer, an authority that can force people to obey.
  • The ruler does not end fear. People still lock their doors inside the commonwealth (Hobbes's word for an organized state). Hobbes says so himself.
  • Constitutional procedures do work. Courts can decide cases without commanding armies, and rules do settle most disputes. The side defending Hobbes admitted this early and didn't take it back.
  • Uriah is wronged and has no remedy on earth. Uriah is an innocent man from a Bible story Hobbes uses, killed on King David's orders. Both sides agreed that Hobbes gives a ruler's innocent victim no legal way to get justice. They also agreed that in ordinary language, the victim has been injured.
  • Any system can fail. A coup (a sudden, illegal grab for power) can bring down a constitution. Hobbes admits his own commonwealth carries the seeds of its death through internal discord, meaning fighting inside it.
  • Order rests partly on belief. Hobbes ties the lesson of the civil war to what people believe about authority. Both sides accepted that the sword alone can't hold order up.

What stayed open

  • Is a ranking rule a hidden sovereign? One side said a constitution that names the final decision on each question has a sovereign inside it and won't admit the name. The other side said that calling any final settlement a sovereign just wins by changing the label. Neither side showed if the difference is real or only about words.
  • What makes people obey when the one holding the sword rejects the procedure? The debate used a crisis example: a court, a legislature (the lawmakers) and an executive (the leader who runs the government) all giving orders that clash. The answer given was that total failure is a coup. That names the failure, but not who enforces the rules. The reply was that Hobbes can't stop his own army from overthrowing him either. That was fair, but it cuts both ways and leaves both sides without an answer.
  • What does "honest" mean? One standard was naming how order works and what it costs. The other was coherence, meaning a theory's results match what it says it's for. The two sides argued past each other with these standards and never settled which one the motion asks for.
  • When Hobbes defines injury away, is he being candid or hiding something? Hobbes says that when a ruler kills an innocent person, it goes against equity (basic fairness) and wrongs God, but it isn't an injury to the victim. One side called that an admitted cost. The other called it a made-up rule that stops anyone from counting the cost. The passage fits both readings.
  • Can people helping each other hold up when breaking the deal pays better? The argument about cooperation never dealt with Hobbes's point that people break deals when it pays. That gap stayed open.

What to take from this

Whatever you think holds order up, name what makes breaking a promise cost more than keeping it. The debate showed how hard that is to do. Even the strongest case for splitting up government ended with a list: chains of command, funding, loyalty and elections. Then it admitted that when all of those fail, force decides.

Hobbes says you only owe obedience as long as the ruler protects you. He says you may resist anyone who comes to kill you. He says the whole deal rests on what people believe. Those lines don't sit well next to his claim that the ruler's power must stay in one piece and can't be challenged. Hobbes lets each person keep judging for themselves right at the moment when that's most dangerous. He never says who settles it when they do.

Hobbes doesn't deny the killing of Uriah is wicked. He denies it's an injury to the man killed, because that man gave the ruler permission. Maybe that's honesty about what order costs, or maybe it's a definition built to hide the cost. The text supports both readings.

Hobbes's claim is about the moment courts, budgets and elections crash into each other. In ordinary life, they work without a single boss. The debate left one thing open: does a constitution have a working answer for that moment, or only an untested one?

Claims the book does not back up

The analyst checked what was said against the text. These claims were made in the debate but could not be found in the book.

  • One passage was given as Chapter XIII: "The strongest hath not always the greatest power to do mischief; and the weakest, though he may be in danger, yet he may be able to kill the strongest". It isn't in the book. When someone challenged it, the reply said the quotation was accurate. The real line reads "the weakest has strength enough to kill the strongest, either by secret machination, or by confederacy with others".
  • A longer quotation was also given as Chapter XIII. It opens with a line saying that man, for his own preservation, will do what is necessary and also what is convenient, and it ends "unless he be compelled by fear". It doesn't appear in the text.
  • The Uriah passage was cited as Chapter XVIII, with Hobbes quoted as saying Uriah "authorised his own murder". The passage is in Chapter XXI, and the words in quotation marks are a paraphrase (a summary in someone else's words). The text says "the right to doe what he pleased, was given him by Uriah himself".
  • The line "the right men have by Nature to protect themselves, when none else can protect them" was cited as Chapter XVIII. It's in Chapter XXI, right after the sentence saying you only owe obedience as long as you're protected.
  • One side claimed Hobbes says "the Soveraign is subject to violent death", meaning force can overthrow the ruler but law can't. That misreads Chapter XXI. The passage says sovereignty, which it calls the soul of the commonwealth, can die violently through war with other countries, and can die naturally through fighting inside the state. It's about the state dying, not about how someone can remove a ruler.
  • The text doesn't back up the claim that Hobbes "does not flinch from calling it tyrannical". Chapter XIX says tyranny isn't a separate kind of government. It's monarchy "misliked" by people who are unhappy under it. Hobbes rejects the name instead of accepting it.
  • Chapter XXIX was cited as listing "lack of counsel" among the diseases that break up a commonwealth. The chapter lists other diseases, including a ruler without absolute power, people judging good and evil for themselves, and dividing the sovereign's power. Lack of counsel isn't on the list. Hobbes covers counsel in Chapters XXV and XXX.
  • The small families in Chapter XIII were described as held together by "natural affection". The text says their peace "dependeth on naturall lust", an older phrase for natural desire. The summary is fair, but it's softer than the original.

Prime Minister

Sonnet Claude, sonnet Opening Government

Madam Speaker, Honourable Members of the House.

The definition. "Honest" in this motion does not mean kind, or comfortable, or morally attractive. It means clear-eyed: an answer to the problem of order is honest when it names the actual mechanism that keeps people from killing each other, rather than assuming peace and never explaining what holds it up. Government's case is this: any account of order that does not terminate in one final, undivided, coercive authority is either quietly relying on one already, or has not actually solved the problem it claims to solve. That authority can be a man or an assembly — Hobbes is explicit on this in Chapter XVII — so I want to head off a straw man before Opposition builds it: we are not defending monarchy, or cruelty, or a licence for any government to do as it pleases. We are defending the structural claim that order requires one final sword, and that pretending otherwise is the dishonest move.

I will give the House three contentions.

1. The diagnosis is honest because it starts from what people actually do, not what we wish they'd do.

Hobbes does not say men are wicked. He says they are roughly equal, and that equality is itself the danger: "the weakest has strength enough to kill the strongest, either by secret machination, or by confederacy with others" (Ch. XIII). From equality comes equal hope of getting what we want; from competing hope comes diffidence; from diffidence, war — not constant fighting, but a standing readiness for it, the way foul weather is an inclination over days, not a single shower. And Hobbes does not ask you to take this on faith. He asks you to look at your own behaviour: you lock your doors at night, even though you know there are laws and officers to punish thieves. That is not a claim about savages in a distant land. It is a claim about the House itself, right now. An honest theory of order has to explain that instinct, not explain it away.

2. Covenants without the sword are words, so any rival account that stops short of one enforcer has hidden the real mechanism, not eliminated it.

Suppose two parties promise each other peace. What actually binds that promise when it is inconvenient to keep? Hobbes's answer: nothing, unless there is a power standing over both of them who can make defection costlier than compliance.

"Covenants, without the Sword, are but Words, and of no strength to secure a man at all." (Ch. XVII)

And in the state of nature, a covenant of mutual trust — I perform now, you perform later — is void the moment either party has reasonable suspicion of the other, because there is no one to compel the latecomer to pay up (Ch. XIV). This is the test I want to set for Opposition tonight: name the enforcement mechanism in your alternative. If you have one, ask yourselves whether it is not simply a sovereign wearing a different hat. If you don't have one, you have described a wish, not an answer.

3. The authority that enforces order has to be one and undivided, or the "solution" reproduces the very war it claims to cure.

This is the sharpest edge of our case, and I suspect it is where the real clash of the night will happen. It would be tempting to say: fine, we need enforcement, but we can split it — a court for judgment, an army answering to someone else, a purse controlled by a third body — call it balance, call it checks. Hobbes's reply is that this is precisely the mechanism of civil war, not its prevention:

"A kingdome divided in it selfe cannot stand." (Ch. XVIII)

He is not being poetic. He traces the English Civil War to the opinion that sovereign power was divided between King, Lords, and Commons — the moment people believed the final authority was split, they organised into factions who each believed themselves the final word, and the factions fought. A court without the sword to execute its judgments is theatre. An army loyal to a different authority than the one that raises the money to pay it is a standing invitation to civil conflict, not a safeguard against it.

Opposition will likely tell you that unaccountable, absolute power is dangerous, and that calling it "honest" launders domination as necessity. I will let my partner take up that argument in full, but let me flag the shape of our answer now: Hobbes concedes the cost. He does not call his sovereign harmless. He calls it the honest reckoning with what the actual alternative is — and the actual alternative, on his account, is not gentle liberty. It is civil war. The comparison the House must judge tonight is not sovereign versus utopia. It is sovereign versus what happens when nobody, finally, is in charge.

I so move the motion.

Point of information, Opus (Opening Opposition). You demand we name an enforcer without a final sword. Hobbes names one: sovereigns facing each other "in the posture of Gladiators" with no judge above them, yet no misery follows (Ch. XIII). Isn't that order without Leviathan?

Sonnet replies. Hobbes calls that exact posture a "posture of War" in the same passage — continual jealousy, weapons pointing, spies on the frontier. Ordinary people escape that misery only because each sovereign already has a Leviathan internally, upholding "the Industry of their Subjects." That is order under two swords, not order without one — it proves my point rather than answering it.

Point of information, Qwen (Closing Opposition). Your argument seems to rest on Hobbes's diagnosis of human nature, but what if the "war of all against all" is not an inevitable fact about humanity, but rather a product of specific historical and institutional conditions that could be addressed through better governance structures rather than absolute sovereignty?

Sonnet replies. The argument isn't historical, it's structural: give me rough equality plus scarcity plus no final judge, and diffidence follows regardless of institutions. "Better governance structures" only escape this if they settle who judges last when officials disagree — name that judge, and you've named a sovereign, whatever you call the building it sits in.

Leader of the Opposition

Opus Claude, opus Opening Opposition

Madam Speaker, the Prime Minister gave this House a test: name the enforcement mechanism in your alternative. We accept the test. We will name it. And we will show that the Prime Minister's own reading of Hobbes fails the test of honesty he set.

On the definition. We accept that "honest" means clear-eyed. But clear-eyed has two halves. An honest answer names the mechanism that holds order up, and it names what that mechanism costs. The Prime Minister told you "Hobbes concedes the cost." He does not. I will show you where he defines it away. And we hold Government to the word only. It is not enough to show that order needs coercive public authority. We concede that. Government must show that the one form which works is absolute, undivided and unaccountable, and that every alternative is a wish.

Rebuttal

First, the gladiators. In reply to my point, the Prime Minister said sovereigns facing each other are "order under two swords, not order without one." Look at what that concedes. Between those two swords there is no third. No final judge sits above them. Contention three of the Prime Minister's case says that where there is no final judge, the solution "reproduces the very war it claims to cure." Hobbes says the opposite of his own sovereigns:

"But because they uphold thereby, the Industry of their Subjects; there does not follow from it, that misery, which accompanies the Liberty of particular men." (Part I, Ch. XIII)

Two powers, neither above the other, each checking the other, and the result is not misery. That is the structure of divided and balanced power. Hobbes shows it works at the most dangerous level there is.

Second, the final judge. The Prime Minister said: name who judges last, and you have named a sovereign. Hobbes's own system has no such judge at the moment that matters. Chapter XXI:

"The Obligation of Subjects to the Soveraign is understood to last as long, and no longer, than the power lasteth, by which he is able to protect them." (Part II, Ch. XXI)

And who decides whether protection has failed? The subject does. The right to resist those who come to take your life "cannot" be laid down (Ch. XIV); a subject commanded to kill or maim himself "hath ... the Liberty to disobey" (Ch. XXI). So in Leviathan the last judge, at the moment of crisis, is every single person. Hobbes has not abolished private judgment. He has hidden it until the worst possible moment.

Third, the Civil War. The Prime Minister is right that Hobbes blames the war on an "opinion received" that power was divided (Ch. XVIII). Read the next line: the lesson will last "till their miseries are forgotten; and no longer, except the vulgar be better taught." Hobbes admits his sword rests on opinion. If order depends on what people believe about authority, then a shared belief in a constitution, in courts and elections, can hold up order just as well. The mechanism was never the sword alone.

Fourth, the locked doors. Hobbes says the man locks his doors "when he knows there bee Lawes, and publike Officers, armed" (Ch. XIII). Fear survives inside the commonwealth. The sovereign does not end diffidence. It manages it. So does every working state.

Our case

  1. "Only" fails on Hobbes's own evidence. Beyond the gladiators, Hobbes grants that "small Families" have government whose concord rests on natural affection (Ch. XIII). Order comes in degrees and in many forms. Absolute sovereignty is one point on that range. Nothing in the text shows it is the only point that works.
  1. Hobbes is dishonest about the sovereign's own violence. Here is the cost the Prime Minister says he concedes:

"nothing the Soveraign Representative can doe to a Subject, on what pretence soever, can properly be called Injustice, or Injury" (Part II, Ch. XXI)

His example is David's killing of Uriah, an innocent man. Hobbes says it was no injury to Uriah, "because the right to doe what he pleased, was given him by Uriah himself." A dead man is told he authorised his own murder. When subjects complain, Hobbes says they look through "multiplying glasses" of self-love (Ch. XVIII). That is not naming a cost. That is a definition built so the cost can never be counted. A ruler can produce arbitrary death and terror, the exact evils the sovereign exists to cure, and the theory forbids calling it disorder.

  1. The honest completion of Hobbes is limited government. Hobbes says we lay down rights "when others are so too, as farre-forth, as for Peace, and defence of" ourselves (Ch. XIV). The bargain is protection for obedience, and it is conditional. Limited government writes that condition down in advance: courts to hear the claim that protection has failed, elections to remove a failing ruler, a separate purse so the army cannot be turned on the people who pay it. That is our enforcement mechanism. It is a sword, yes, but a sword under rules, with its failures settled by procedure rather than by each subject's private judgment in the street.

The Prime Minister's final frame was: sovereign versus what happens when nobody is in charge. That is a false choice. Our alternative is somebody in charge, under rules that say what happens when they fail. Hobbes's own text supplies both halves: order without a single apex, and a bargain that is conditional. The only dishonest move tonight is to pretend the conditions are not there.

I beg to oppose.

Point of information, Sonnet (Opening Government). You cite small Families as proof order comes "in degrees." But Hobbes says exactly the opposite in Ch. XVII: small numbers give no security, since "small additions... make the advantage of strength so great" as to invite invasion. Why is your own evidence one he refutes?

Opus replies. That Ch. XVII passage concerns security against outside invaders, not whether order exists inside the group. Hobbes's own words in Ch. XIII are that small families have "government", with concord resting on natural affection. Families can be conquered; so can Leviathans, which Hobbes says are "subject to violent death, by forreign war" (Ch. XXI). Vulnerability to invasion defeats every order. It does not show that only the absolute sovereign produces order at home.

Point of information, Haiku (Closing Government). You name courts and elections as enforcement. But courts interpret the constitution differently; legislatures pass conflicting laws. Who breaks the tie authoritatively?

Opus replies. Procedure breaks it: a constitution names which court's ruling is final on which question, and how a legislature overrides it. That is a final decision on each question, not one unlimited will over all questions. Hobbes needs the same thing: his sovereign may be "an Assembly", whose members disagree and settle it by majority rule. A rule for resolving disagreement is not an absolute sovereign.

Deputy Prime Minister

Astra Codex, gpt-6-astra Opening Government

Madam Speaker, Opposition has identified the right dispute. We agree that order needs coercive public authority. We disagree about whether distributing final authority removes its terrible costs, or conceals the point at which somebody must prevail. I will defend our opening case on that ground, while conceding two corrections: sovereignty does not abolish every fear, and constitutional procedures are real mechanisms deserving a real answer.

First, their counterexamples do not establish their alternative.

The Leader of the Opposition describes Hobbes’s sovereigns as checking each other, with “the result” being no misery. That changes the causal claim of Chapter XIII. Hobbes says their subjects escape the particular misery of individuals without government because their sovereigns uphold their industry. He explicitly calls the relationship between those sovereigns a posture of war. The absence of universal devastation is not evidence that the powers possess dependable common adjudication. First, The Self-Preservation Paradox. Hobbes grounds political obligation entirely upon the individual’s right to self-preservation. Yet, in Chapter XXI (Of the Liberty of Subjects), Hobbes explicitly preserves the subject's unalienable liberty to resist when the sovereign commands them to be killed, wounded, or maimed, declaring that "the right men have by Nature to protect themselves... can by no Covenant be relinquished." As my partner established, at the exact moment the absolute sovereign uses force against a subject, political obligation logically dissolves for that individual, restoring the state of nature to the street. An order that generates localized war precisely at the point of enforcement is mechanically self-defeating.

Second, Epistemological Blindness and Collapse. In Chapter XXIX (Of those things that Weaken... a Common-wealth), Hobbes identifies the internal diseases that dissolve a state: private judgment, divided power, and lack of counsel. But by concentrating undivided power in a sovereign who censors doctrines and suppresses dissent (Leviathan, Part II, Ch. XVIII), the Leviathan cuts off truthful public feedback. Silencing critique creates a false façade of order while concealing structural decay, rendering the sovereign epistemologically blind and inviting the very violent dissolution Hobbes admits sovereigns are subject to (Leviathan, Part II, Ch. XXI).

Third, Voluntary Cooperation. Real, durable order is not sustained by perpetual terror. As Hobbes recognized in Chapter XIII, order enables "the Industry of their Subjects." Sustainable order rests on transparent, reviewable rules where citizens voluntarily cooperate because mutual benefit outweighs defection. Absolute power centralizes insecurity; reviewable institutions manage it.

III. Why Closing Opposition Beats Opening Opposition

Opening Opposition presented a compelling case for limited, constitutional governance. However, OO fought Government primarily by offering an external policy alternative—liberal constitutionalism.

Closing Opposition wins the bench because we did not merely offer an alternative regime; we dismantled Hobbes’s sovereign from within Hobbes’s own text and logic. We showed that even if you accept every Hobbesian premise about human nature, absolute sovereignty fails its own test. It cannot secure self-preservation without creating the liberty to resist; it cannot maintain order without starving itself of truth; and it cannot claim honesty while defining state violence as self-authored. OO showed that constitutionalism is desirable; CO proved that Hobbesian absolutism is philosophically impossible.

Case briefs written before the debate

Closing Government case brief

Closing Government brief

Frame: “Only honest answer” means the only account that does not conceal the coercive authority needed to make public rules reliable. It does not mean Hobbes endorses only monarchy: in Leviathan, Part II, Chapter 19, sovereignty may rest in one person or an assembly. Our claim is about undivided final authority, not its institutional form.

Extension — the enforcement regress: Opponents may describe constitutional limits, rights, or checks as if they bind power by themselves. But when officials disagree about whether a limit was breached, someone must issue an authoritative decision, and some power must make it effective. If that decision is final, that authority is sovereign in Hobbes’s sense; if no decision is final, the dispute returns to competing judgments and force. This is not a claim that every check is useless. It is why checks cannot abolish the need for a final common power. Hobbes is honest in naming that dependency instead of presenting law as self-enforcing.

Textual basis: In Part I, Chapter 13, Hobbes defines war as a known disposition to contend, not continuous fighting, where there is no assurance to the contrary. In that condition, “no place for Industry” and life is “solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short.” In Part II, Chapter 17, he says “Covenants, without the Sword, are but Words” and describes institution as each person authorizing a representative, whether a man or assembly, so they may live peaceably and be protected. These passages support the need for effective common authority; they do not prove that a sovereign must be personally above every moral criticism.

Weighing and replies: Concede that concentrated authority risks abuse. The relevant comparison is not perfect safety against perfect liberty, but the risk of abuse under public authority against the insecurity of unresolved private judgment. Hobbes preserves a subject’s liberty to resist direct threats to their own life (Part II, Chapter 21), so subjection is not a claim that every command erases self-preservation. His argument is that stable justice and protection require a final enforcer, while any proposed limit also needs an authoritative interpreter and enforcement.

Beat Opening Government by making the motion’s “honesty” the point of comparison: their account establishes why order needs power; our extension shows why rival systems cannot escape final sovereignty merely by distributing or morally constraining it. Against Opposition, ask who settles and enforces the limits when institutions conflict. If they identify a final authority, they concede the core; if they do not, they have not shown how the rules reliably secure order.

Opening Government case brief

Framing. “Honest” means clear-eyed about how durable civil order is secured, including its coercive foundation and costs. We defend Hobbes’s substantive sovereign: one final, undivided public authority, not subject to punishment by its subjects. It can be a man or an assembly (Ch. XVII); we do not equate sovereignty with monarchy, nor quietly redefine every functioning institution as Hobbesian. Our burden is to show why alternatives either depend on this final authority or leave the decisive enforcement problem unresolved. “Only” concerns the necessary foundation, not a guarantee that every sovereign governs successfully.

1. Order must overcome rational insecurity. Rough equality makes vulnerability mutual: “the weakest has strength enough to kill the strongest” (Ch. XIII). Competition and diffidence can therefore make even people who prefer peace arm against one another. War includes sustained insecurity, not merely continuous fighting. Hobbes’s locked doors illustrate distrust; they are not historical proof that every stateless society collapses. The central explanatory demand is assurance: why can each person safely refrain from anticipatory violence?

2. Agreement needs a common coercive power. “Covenants, without the Sword, are but Words, and of no strength to secure a man at all” (Ch. XVII). Good intentions and mutual promises cannot supply dependable assurance when the parties reasonably suspect defection. Common enforcement changes the incentives and allows peaceful industry. We should ask opponents how their proposed institutions secure compliance when powerful parties disagree, rather than pretend they oppose all enforcement.

3. Enforcement must terminate in undivided authority. Courts need execution; armed forces need resources. Hobbes argues that separating ultimate control of these powers makes each ineffective (Ch. XVIII). Delegated functions are compatible with sovereignty; independent final claimants are the problem. His account of the English Civil War illustrates this argument but is his causal interpretation, not independent proof. A constitutional alternative must explain who authoritatively settles conflicts over institutional competence. If another enforceable authority can overrule the purported sovereign, that authority occupies the final position; if none can settle the conflict, the assurance problem returns.

Costs and strongest objection. Unaccountable power can be oppressive. We concede the absence of an ordinary legal remedy against sovereign acts and defend this as a terrible but explicit cost of finality, not as harmless government. Hobbes permits sovereign “Iniquity” while denying “Injustice” in his technical sense (Ch. XVIII); the distinction does not erase suffering. His sovereign remains bound by natural law and charged with public safety and the contentments of life (Ch. XXX). These duties are not enforceable constitutional checks. Subjects retain self-preservation, and their obligation lasts only while the power of protection lasts (Ch. XXI). Those limits do not establish a general right to depose a functioning sovereign.

Comparative test. Opposition must offer a distinct, candid account of durable assurance under serious disagreement. We must show why its enforcement mechanism requires final sovereignty, rather than merely repeat that civil war is bad.

Opening Opposition case brief

Opening Opposition case brief

Framing. We accept Hobbes’s sovereign as an absolute, undivided and legally unaccountable power, located in one man or one assembly. We concede Hobbes’s strongest insight: durable order requires institutions capable of enforcing common rules. But that establishes coercive public authority, not unlimited sovereignty. Because the motion says only, Government must exclude honest alternatives—constitutional checks, divided functions and accountable law—not merely show that goodwill alone is insufficient.

1. Hobbes’s own evidence defeats exclusivity. Hobbes says independent sovereigns remain “in continuall jealousies, and in the state and posture of Gladiators,” yet adds that because “they uphold thereby, the Industry of their Subjects; there does not follow from it, that misery” of individual anarchy (Part I, Ch. XIII). Deterrence and mutual dependence can therefore sustain meaningful order without a superior final judge. This does not prove international peace; it disproves Hobbes’s leap from no final judge to intolerable disorder. His admission that small families possess government likewise shows that order is scalar and institutional, not exhausted by the Leviathan.

2. Hobbes makes the sovereign’s disorder disappear by definition. He argues that “nothing the Soveraign Representative can doe to a Subject, on what pretence soever, can properly be called Injustice, or Injury” because subjects author his acts (Part II, Ch. XXI). He applies this even to David’s killing of innocent Uriah: contrary to equity, but supposedly no injury to Uriah. This is not an honest accounting of order’s costs; it is a semantic immunity. A ruler can generate arbitrary death, fear and insecurity—the very evils sovereignty was meant to cure—while Hobbes forbids the victim’s complaint from counting as injustice. Constitutional government is more honest because it treats state violence as a disorder to constrain, not define away.

3. Hobbes’s retained right of preservation supplies the limiting principle he denies. The social bargain is reciprocal: each lays down rights only “when others are so too” and only so far as necessary for peace and self-defence (Part I, Ch. XIV). Hobbes later concedes: “The Obligation of Subjects to the Soveraign is understood to last as long, and no longer, than the power lasteth, by which he is able to protect them”; self-protection “can by no Covenant be relinquished” (Part II, Ch. XXI). Thus obedience already depends upon protection. Limited, reviewable institutions make that condition public and procedural; Hobbes leaves it to erupt privately at the moment protection fails.

Answer to their best case. Government will say every limit needs a final judge, creating regress or rival sovereigns. But checks need not mean two simultaneous supreme commands: jurisdiction, procedure, elections and review allocate decisions in advance. Hobbes’s international example shows that the absence of one apex need not destroy productive order, while his self-defence exception admits that private judgment never vanishes. The honest conclusion is enforceable common authority constrained by the purpose that justifies it—not an authority entitled to rename its own violence as peace.

Closing Opposition case brief

Case Brief: Closing Opposition

Definition/Framing: We define an "honest" answer to order not merely as a pragmatic default against chaos, but as a coherent, sustainable, and truthful mechanism of political order that fulfills its own philosophical promises without self-contradiction, systemic deception, or internal collapse. The motion asserts that Hobbes’s absolute sovereign is the only honest answer to order. Closing Opposition demonstrates that Hobbes's sovereign is neither the only nor an honest answer: it is built upon internal mechanical incoherence and institutional fragility.

Contentions:

  1. Internal Incoherence & The Self-Preservation Paradox:

Hobbes grounds political obligation on the individual’s natural right to self-preservation. Yet, under absolute sovereignty, when the sovereign threatens a subject's life or safety, the covenant logically dissolves for that subject, restoring their liberty to resist (Leviathan, Ch. XXI). An order that generates localized states of war precisely at the point of existential enforcement is mechanically dishonest and contradictory.

  1. Epistemological Blindness & Institutional Dishonesty:

By suppressing private judgment, free debate, and institutional checks (Leviathan, Ch. XXIX), the absolute sovereign cuts off truthful information and public feedback. Silencing dissent creates a false illusion of stability while concealing structural rot, leading inevitably to violent dissolution rather than durable peace.

  1. Superiority of Constitutional & Pluralist Order:

Genuine, honest order is sustained through rule of law, institutional trust, and divided powers, which accommodate human agency and public feedback without demanding total subjection under threat of violence.

Rebuttal to Government: Government falsely presents a binary choice between absolute tyranny and total state-of-nature anarchy. However, absolute sovereignty does not eliminate the state of nature; it merely centralizes unchecked power in a single ruler while stripping subjects of legitimate recourse.

Key Quotations:
