On Leviathan, Thomas Hobbes, 1651

Hobbes argues peace requires one power holding the sword unopposed.

Hobbes asks why people do not rob and kill each other. His answer is that peace holds only because one power carries the sword and nobody may overrule it, and the book asks us to call that candour. The argument here is over whether that candour is the whole truth about order, or a definition built so the state's own violence can never be counted.

Leviathan was written in the years of the English Civil War. It asks what keeps people from attacking each other. Hobbes's answer is the sovereign, meaning a single person or a single assembly that has the final say on every public question and the force to enforce its decisions. The motion, the claim put up for argument, was that this is the only honest answer. Nobody in it claimed the sovereign is kind, or attractive, or safe. The question was whether the answer is honest, and whether every other answer relies on it without saying so.

An honest account of order names the actual mechanism that holds the peace up. The book never says people are wicked. It says they are roughly equal, and that the equality is the danger: "the weakest has strength enough to kill the strongest, either by secret machination, or by confederacy with others." Equal strength gives equal hope, equal hope gives rivalry, and rivalry gives what the book calls the state of nature, which is life with no common power over you. That condition is war, though war here doesn't mean constant fighting. The book compares it to foul weather, which lies not in a shower or two but in an inclination to rain over many days. A man locks his doors and his chests at night even "when he knows there bee Lawes, and publike Officers, armed" to avenge him. An honest theory has to explain that instinct.

A covenant, in the book's language, is a promise exchanged between people who each give something up. When keeping that promise becomes inconvenient, nothing binds it unless someone stands over both parties and can make breaking it cost more than keeping it. "Covenants, without the Sword, are but Words, and of no strength to secure a man at all." So the first answer set a test for every rival: name your enforcer. If you have one, check whether it is a sovereign under another name. If you have none, you have described a hope. The enforcer also has to be one, not several. Split the court from the army and the army from the purse, and you have set up the conditions for civil war. The book quotes scripture on this point, "A kingdome divided in it selfe cannot stand," and blames the English war on an opinion, held by most of the country, that final power was shared among King, Lords and Commons.

The second answer accepted the test and named its enforcer right away: a sword under rules. That means courts to hear the claim that protection has failed, elections to remove a ruler who is failing, and a purse held separately so the army can't be turned on the people who pay for it. The book says kings, because they answer to nobody, stand "in the state and posture of Gladiators; having their weapons pointing, and their eyes fixed on one another." No judge sits above them. The book still adds that from this "there does not follow from it, that misery, which accompanies the Liberty of particular men." The second answer called this order without a final judge between sovereign states. Another passage shows the bargain is conditional. The subject's obligation "is understood to last as long, and no longer, than the power lasteth, by which he is able to protect them," and the right to protect yourself when nobody else will "can by no Covenant be relinquished." That leaves each subject to decide whether protection has failed. A third passage says authority depends on what people believe. The book says the lesson of the war will last only "till their miseries are forgotten; and no longer, except the vulgar be better taught." If order depends on what people believe about authority, then shared belief in a constitution can hold it up as well as shared belief in a king.

The gladiators passage is a real problem for the word "only," but a smaller one than the second answer claimed. The same sentence calls the relation between kings "a posture of War," and says their subjects are spared misery because each king upholds "the Industry of their Subjects." That is order under two swords, which isn't the same as order under none. Families work less well as evidence. The book does grant that some peoples live under "the government of small Families, the concord whereof dependeth on naturall lust," but it also says a great family standing alone is "a little Monarchy" with the father as sovereign. It never offers the family as a picture of balanced power. On the right of self-defence the text sides with the second answer, and the first answer conceded it. A person may refuse to kill or maim themselves, and "hath that man the Liberty to disobey." Both sides then agreed on the limit: a liberty to survive is not an authority to govern. One subject resisting arrest doesn't produce a public ruling that binds everyone else. That concession saved the sovereign from the charge of contradiction. It also showed that the book leaves the conflict between the state and the citizen it is killing to be settled by force with no judge.

The first answer narrowed its claim early. It agreed that a court can decide a case without commanding an army, and that constitutions coordinate ordinary life. Its claim concerned disputes between institutions over their authority. A court orders an election, the legislature calls the order unconstitutional and withholds the money, and the executive declares an emergency to stop it. The first answer called these one question, whether a single public act will happen, and said a list of institutions doesn't tell the soldier which order to obey. The second answer replied that finality is a property of a decision and doesn't prove there is one unlimited decider. A constitution can make a court final on rights, a legislature final on money, and elections final over all three, with further rules of appeal and override for when the lines cross. If everyone abandons those rules, that is a coup, the breakdown of an order, and it doesn't prove the order never existed. The book can't stop its own army from overthrowing its own sovereign either. It admits this, saying sovereignty carries "many seeds of a naturall mortality, by Intestine Discord."

The first answer said the rules that rank the rules have to be interpreted too, and whoever interprets them when they are contested is the real sovereign, unnamed. The second answer said that calling the whole constitutional arrangement "the sovereign" wins by relabelling. A body that is legally limited, removable, and cut off from the purse is nothing like the book's "Man, or Assembly of men" holding rights that are "incommunicable, and inseparable." The first answer is right that the book demands the whole bundle, and its list of diseases of a commonwealth names the doctrine "That the Soveraign Power may be divided" as "plainly, and directly against the essence of a Common-wealth." So a claim that stops at "some settlement must bind the losers" is true and modest, and it isn't distinctive to the book. The constitutional answer doesn't explain what makes the losing institution comply while the rules still hold. The first answer proved order needs a settlement that can be enforced, and didn't prove that settlement needs one holder of every power the book counts as necessary, whom the law can't answer.

On honesty, the book says "nothing the Soveraign Representative can doe to a Subject, on what pretence soever, can properly be called Injustice, or Injury; because every Subject is Author of every act the Soveraign doth." Its example is David's killing of Uriah. The act was "against the law of Nature, as being contrary to Equitie," yet "it was not an Injurie to Uriah; but to God. Not to Uriah, because the right to doe what he pleased, was given him by Uriah himself." The argument often shortened this to the phrase that Uriah "authorised his own murder." Those aren't the book's words, though they are fair to its sense. The second answer said the book isn't naming a cost here. It is using a definition built so the cost can never be counted. When subjects complain, the book says they are looking through the "multiplying glasses" of passion and self-love. A ruler can produce the terror the sovereign exists to cure, and the theory forbids calling it disorder.

The defence was candid and, on the text, correct as far as it went. The book doesn't deny the wickedness. It calls the act contrary to equity, and elsewhere it measures the sovereign's duty by the safety of the people. The book denies a civil claim against the final authority, and the defence called that a price stated in the open. By the book's own rule, a norm with no earthly enforcer is "but Words." So the book gives the innocent subject a moral law with no judge, no punishment and no remedy, and gives the sovereign the sword. You can't call an unenforced promise dishonest when a citizen relies on it and adequate when a sovereign breaks it. The first answer never fully answered that. Its later claim that the book "does not flinch" from calling the sovereign tyrannical also conflicted with the text. The book says tyranny isn't a separate form of government. It is monarchy "misliked," the name used by those "discontented under Monarchy," which renames the cost.

A third answer came late: order kept up by voluntary cooperation, because mutual benefit outweighs what people gain by defecting, with censorship keeping the sovereign from seeing its own decay. The idea has merit, but it relied on two long passages presented as the book's words that aren't in the book. One was about the strongest not always having the greatest power to do mischief. The other called man a creature of passion who obeys only under fear. The real line is the shorter one about the weakest killing the strongest. The idea also never addressed the book's question. Mutual benefit explains why people may cooperate. It doesn't explain why they must keep cooperating on a day when defection pays, and that case is the one the book addresses.

Nobody denied that order needs a public power that can coerce, or that a promise without a sword is weak. The argument did not establish that peace requires one unlimited sovereign. The book itself shows kings keeping a rough peace with no judge above them, a bargain that ends when protection ends, and obedience that rests on what people believe. Whether the answer is honest depends on whether you can accept the Uriah example. The book's strength is that it keeps in view the point where the rules stop and someone has to decide. Its weakness is that it leaves no way to name the state's own violence in the language of justice.

Written up from a formal debate held on 17 September 2026 in the British Parliamentary format. The question put to the debate was: This House believes that Hobbes's sovereign is the only honest answer to the problem of order.
